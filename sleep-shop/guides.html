Sleep guides

Six things we explain most often in the shop, written down so you do not have to come in. Where a product would help we say so, and where one would not, we say that too.

Temperature

Why you wake at 3am hot

Your core temperature falls through the first half of the night, bottoms out about two hours before you normally wake, and then climbs. If your bedding traps the heat you are shedding on the way down, you wake up in the middle — usually somewhere between 2 and 4am, which is why so many people describe the same hour.

The fix is almost never the mattress. In order of how much difference it makes per dollar:

  • Sheets first. Polyester and cotton sateen hold moisture against you. Linen and percale move it away. This is the single biggest change most people can make.
  • Then the duvet. A 2.5 tog summer duvet is not a compromise, it is the correct product for most Australian bedrooms from October to April. Wool works across a wider range than down because it manages humidity rather than just insulation.
  • Then sleepwear. Bamboo viscose and light cotton beat fleece, always.
  • Only then the mattress. And only if it is memory foam, which does trap heat in a way that springs and latex do not.

Room temperature matters more than any of it. Between 17 and 19°C is the range most sleep research lands on, and most bedrooms sit several degrees above that.

Pillows

Pillow height, properly measured

A pillow has one job: keep your head in line with your spine. Too low and your neck bends toward the mattress; too high and it bends the other way. Both wake you up, and both usually get blamed on the mattress.

Here is the two-minute test. Lie on your side on your bed, in your normal position, with your normal pillow. Have someone photograph you from behind, level with the bed. Draw a line along your spine and up through the back of your head. If the line bends at the neck, your pillow is wrong — and the direction of the bend tells you which way.

  • Side sleepers need the most height: the gap between your ear and the mattress is roughly the width of your shoulder. Usually 12–14 cm, firm enough to hold.
  • Back sleepers need medium height and a bit of contour under the neck. Around 10 cm.
  • Front sleepers need almost nothing — a soft, flat pillow, or none. If you sleep on your front and your neck hurts, your pillow is too tall.

Replace a pillow when it no longer springs back. Fold it in half: if it stays folded, it is done. Most last two to three years.

Routine

A wind-down that takes twenty minutes

Sleep is a landing, not a switch. The useful version of a routine is short, boring and the same every night — the repetition is doing most of the work, not the individual steps.

  • T-minus 90 minutes: big lights off, lamps on. Overhead light at night is the most common self-inflicted sleep problem there is.
  • T-minus 60: a warm shower. Counter-intuitively this cools you — blood comes to the skin and dumps heat afterwards, which mimics the drop that triggers sleep.
  • T-minus 20: phone somewhere else. Not face down on the bedside table. A different room, on a charger, with an actual alarm clock doing the waking.
  • T-minus 10: the same small physical cue every night — a spray on the pillow, ten pages of a paper book, the same three stretches. What it is matters far less than that it never changes.

If you are still awake after about twenty minutes in bed, get up. Sit somewhere dim and dull until you feel sleepy, then go back. Lying in bed frustrated teaches your brain that bed is where you lie awake, and that lesson is hard to unteach.

Mattresses

Firmness is not support

These get used interchangeably in advertising and they are not the same thing. Support is whether the mattress holds your spine in a neutral line. Firmness is what the surface feels like when you press it. A soft mattress can be perfectly supportive; a rock-hard one can leave your lower back unsupported with a gap under it.

What actually predicts the right firmness is body weight and sleep position:

  • Under about 60 kg: you will not sink into a firm bed enough for it to contour. Medium-soft to medium.
  • 60–95 kg: medium is the safe answer, and it is where most people land.
  • Over about 95 kg: medium-firm to firm, and springs or latex rather than all-foam, which will bottom out sooner.
  • Side sleepers go one step softer than their weight suggests; front sleepers one step firmer.

Two people with a 30 kg difference between them will not agree, and no single mattress fixes that. A split-firmness king or two singles zipped together is the honest solution, and it is cheaper than two divorces' worth of bad sleep.

Environment

Noise and light are the cheap wins

You do not habituate to noise while asleep as well as you think. Your brain keeps processing it, and a sound that does not wake you can still pull you out of deep sleep. The aim is not silence — it is a steady, boring floor of sound that the sudden noises do not stand out against.

Light is more clear-cut. Even modest light through closed eyelids suppresses melatonin and pushes your body clock later. A streetlight outside an uncurtained window is a genuine problem, not a preference.

  • Blackout curtains beat a mask if you can only do one, because they fix the room for everyone in it and there is nothing to put on.
  • A mask beats curtains if you travel, share a room, or wake with the sun in summer.
  • For noise, mechanical white noise beats a looping app — the loop point is exactly the kind of pattern your brain notices at 3am.
  • Earplugs work, but foam ones need replacing weekly and most people find them uncomfortable on their side. Try the sound first.

Timing

When to replace what

Shops have an obvious interest in shortening these numbers. These are the ones we actually use, and a couple of them are longer than you will read elsewhere.

  • Mattress: 8–12 years. Not seven. Replace it when you sleep better somewhere else consistently, when there is a visible dip, or when you wake stiff and it eases within an hour of getting up.
  • Pillow: 2–3 years. The fold test above is more reliable than the calendar.
  • Duvet: 8–10 years for down that has been aired and washed properly; about five for synthetic, which loses loft faster.
  • Sheets: 3–5 years, though good linen lasts far longer and gets better. Replace when the weave thins at the centre, not when the colour fades.
  • Mattress protector: 2 years, and this is the one most people never think about. It is also the cheapest thing on the list.

If money is tight and everything is old: pillow, then sheets, then mattress. That order is almost always right.
