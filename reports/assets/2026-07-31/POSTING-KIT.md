## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; optionally attach slide 1)

Rebel Wilson just gave every media lawyer in Australia a perfect teaching case.

On 22 July, Justice Raper of the Federal Court dismissed Charlotte MacInnes' defamation and breach of confidence claims against Wilson, brought over four sets of Instagram posts published between September 2024 and July 2025 about a dispute on the set of The Deb. An appeal has already been filed — but the first-instance reasoning is worth studying regardless of what happens next.

Three doctrinal points stand out.

First, imputations are decided objectively. Of all the meanings MacInnes alleged, the Court found only one was actually conveyed by any post: that she had changed her account of the incident. Plaintiffs frame their case at its highest; courts read posts the way ordinary readers do. Most defamation cases are won or lost right here.

Second, substantial truth did its job. Justice Raper found the single conveyed meaning — that MacInnes changed her story — was substantially true. Truth remains the complete defence, and it was squarely established on the evidence.

Third, and most importantly for practitioners: serious harm. Since the 2021 reforms, a plaintiff must prove the publication caused, or was likely to cause, serious harm to their reputation. MacInnes failed to establish it. The days of assuming harm from publication are over — even for posts by a global celebrity to a mass audience.

For anyone advising clients tempted to litigate over social media commentary: this case is the checklist. What was actually conveyed? Is it provably false? And can you evidence serious harm — not hurt feelings, harm to reputation?

And for public figures tempted to post "their side" of a brewing dispute: Wilson posted, got sued, and won on truth. That's a high-wire act, not a template — but it worked.
