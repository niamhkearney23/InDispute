**LinkedIn post:**

The first US appellate decision on fair use in AI training arrived this week, and the AI developer lost.

The case: Thomson Reuters Enterprise Centre GmbH v ROSS Intelligence Inc.

The facts are unglamorous and that is precisely why they matter. ROSS Intelligence built an AI legal research platform. Thomson Reuters, owner of Westlaw, sued over the use of material derived from its headnotes to train that tool. The District of Delaware issued a revised opinion rejecting the fair use defence. On 29 September 2026 the US Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit affirmed.

Two features deserve attention.

First, the opinion was issued under seal. An appellate court has resolved the most contested question in AI law right now, and the reasoning is not yet available to the profession. Reporting indicates competition was central to the analysis, but reporting is not a judgment, and practitioners should resist treating it as one.

Second, look at who showed up. The RIAA and the NMPA backed Thomson Reuters. This was never really a dispute about legal research. It is a dispute about whether copying a catalogue to train a model that competes with that catalogue can be excused as transformative.

The doctrinal takeaway is the one students should carry into an exam: the fourth fair use factor, market effect, is doing the work. A use that produces a market substitute for the copied material, or that undercuts an emerging licensing market for training data, is in serious trouble no matter how technically novel the process.

For in-house teams, the practical question has shifted. It is no longer &ldquo;is our training set transformative&rdquo;. It is &ldquo;does our output compete with the people whose material we trained on, and have we licensed accordingly&rdquo;.

When the reasoning is finally unsealed, which part will you read first: the factor four analysis, or the treatment of the licensing market for training data?
