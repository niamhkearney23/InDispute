The High Court delivered O&rsquo;Connell v Director of Public Prosecutions; Director of Public Prosecutions v O&rsquo;Connell [2026] HCA 41 on 4 November 2026, before Gageler CJ, Gordon, Edelman, Steward, Gleeson, Jagot and Beech-Jones JJ.

The facts in brief. A jury in the Supreme Court of the Australian Capital Territory found Michael O&rsquo;Connell guilty of murder. A majority of the ACT Court of Appeal, Loukas-Karlsson and Taylor JJ, with McCallum CJ dissenting, upheld his unreasonable verdict ground, set aside the guilty verdict and the sentence, and entered a verdict of not guilty of murder. In a second judgment on 29 September 2025, the same majority entered a verdict of guilty of manslaughter and remitted the proceeding to the trial judge for resentencing. McCallum CJ would have ordered a retrial on manslaughter.

Two appeals came to the High Court. The first asked whether it was open to the appellate court to substitute a verdict of guilty of manslaughter, including the effect of s 297 of the Crimes Act 1900 (ACT). The second asked whether the jury&rsquo;s verdict was unreasonable or not supported by the evidence, including whether the jury ought to have entertained a doubt about the mental element of reckless indifference.

The outcome. Mr O&rsquo;Connell&rsquo;s appeal was dismissed. The Director&rsquo;s appeal was allowed. The Court held that, in upholding the contention that the murder conviction was unreasonable, the majority of the Court of Appeal erred in failing to make full allowance for all the advantages enjoyed by the jury, and further erred in failing to consider all the circumstances established by the evidence and to weigh them in determining whether an inference consistent with innocence was reasonably open.

The practical point for anyone who appears in criminal appeals, or who is about to sit an exam on them: the unreasonable verdict ground is not an invitation to re-run the trial on the transcript. The advantages of the jury are not a formality to be noted and passed over, and the circumstances in the evidence have to be assessed together rather than picked apart one by one.
