Hidden cameras. An abattoir. And a unanimous High Court lesson in restraint.

On 11 November 2026 the High Court decided Farm Transparency International Ltd v The Game Meats Company of Australia Pty Ltd [2026] HCA 42.

The activist group trespassed on Game Meats&rsquo; abattoir, installed hidden cameras, and trespassed again to retrieve them. The aim was footage of animal treatment, and publication of it.

The primary judge awarded general and exemplary damages, but refused an injunction against publication, refused a permanent deletion order, and held that the copyright in the images was not held on constructive trust.

The Full Court of the Federal Court went the other way. It declared a constructive trust over that copyright, which gave Game Meats a route to a legal assignment and to remedies against third party publishers, plus injunctions and deletion orders on top.

The High Court, sitting seven, allowed the appeal in part. Damages were inadequate to rectify the consequences of the trespasses. But the only orders equity needed were orders requiring Farm Transparency to delete or destroy all images and copies obtained from the trespasses.

The principle to carry into an exam: inadequacy of damages is the gateway to equitable relief, not a blank cheque for the widest remedy on the menu.

A useful remedies case landed yesterday, and it is one every junior litigator should read.

Farm Transparency International Ltd v The Game Meats Company of Australia Pty Ltd [2026] HCA 42, decided 11 November 2026 before Gageler CJ, Gordon, Edelman, Steward, Gleeson, Jagot and Beech-Jones JJ.

The facts are stark. Farm Transparency wilfully trespassed on Game Meats&rsquo; abattoir, installed hidden cameras, and trespassed again to remove them. The purpose was to obtain footage of the treatment of animals and publish it.

At first instance in the Federal Court, Game Meats recovered general and exemplary damages. The primary judge declined to enjoin publication of the images, declined to order permanent deletion, and held the activists&rsquo; copyright in the images was not held on constructive trust.

The Full Court of the Federal Court allowed Game Meats&rsquo; appeal and declared a constructive trust over the copyright. That declaration did real work: it gave Game Meats a vehicle to obtain a legal assignment of the copyright and potential remedies against any third party who published the images, with ancillary injunctions and deletion orders attached.

The High Court unanimously allowed the appeal in part. Damages were inadequate to rectify the consequences of the wrongdoing. Even so, the only orders needed in equity to address the adverse consequences to Game Meats were orders ensuring that Farm Transparency delete or destroy all images, and copies, obtained from the trespasses.

Two takeaways for practice.

First, inadequacy of damages gets you through the door. It does not dictate how far the court will go once you are inside.

Second, the proprietary remedy is the heavy artillery. If a narrower order rectifies the consequences of the wrong, expect the court to prefer it, and expect to be asked why anything more is necessary.
