## The five lines

1. Name, court, year.
2. Only the facts that mattered.
3. The issue, written as a question.
4. The answer, one sentence.
5. The rule you can use again.

Slide 7 works the whole thing through on Donoghue v Stevenson, which is already verified from the negligence post, so nothing new needed checking.

## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

A case note is five lines. Not five pages.

**One. Name, court, year.** Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, House of Lords. Put it first every time, because which court decided it tells you how much weight it carries before you read a word of reasoning.

**Two. Only the facts that mattered.** Not the whole story. The two or three facts the outcome actually turned on. Here is the test: remove a fact. If the answer stays the same, it does not belong in the note.

**Three. The issue, as a question.** "This case is about duty of care" is a topic, not an issue. A real issue ends in a question mark and can be answered yes or no. Does a manufacturer owe a duty to a consumer it has no contract with?

**Four. The answer. One sentence.** What the court held and which way it went. If you cannot say it in one sentence, you have not finished reading it.

**Five. The rule you can use again.** The part that decides the next case, not the part about this one. Everything else the judge said in passing is interesting, and it is not binding.

Worked through, that is:

1. Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562, House of Lords.
2. A woman drank ginger beer bought for her by a friend. The bottle was opaque. It contained a decomposed snail.
3. Does a manufacturer owe a duty to a consumer it has no contract with?
4. Yes.
5. You owe a duty to those so closely and directly affected that you ought to have them in contemplation.

Five lines you understand beat five pages you copied.
