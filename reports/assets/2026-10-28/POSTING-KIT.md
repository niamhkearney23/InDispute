A short costs ruling in the Federal Court this week carries a longer lesson than its length suggests.

In Janssen-Cilag Pty Ltd v Arrotex Pharmaceuticals Pty Ltd (Interlocutory Costs) [2026] FCA 1611, decided by Jackman J on 27 October 2026, the Court dealt with the costs of an interlocutory application that had been brought by non-parties to the proceeding. The application was withdrawn in the course of oral argument. The Court&rsquo;s published catchwords record a finding that the non-parties acted unreasonably in commencing and pursuing it.

The practical takeaway is simple. If you step into someone else&rsquo;s litigation to ask a court for something, you step into that court&rsquo;s costs jurisdiction with it. Pulling the application halfway through oral argument does not reset the position, because the court can still look at whether it should have been brought and pursued at all.

For students: this is a clean, quotable example of the costs discretion operating against people whose names are not on the originating process. Worth a line in your civil procedure notes.

**LinkedIn post:**

There is a quiet assumption behind a lot of interlocutory strategy: that if an application starts to go badly, you withdraw it, and the problem goes away.

A Federal Court decision published this week is a reminder that it does not always work that way.

In Janssen-Cilag Pty Ltd v Arrotex Pharmaceuticals Pty Ltd (Interlocutory Costs) [2026] FCA 1611, Jackman J dealt on 27 October 2026 with the costs of an interlocutory application brought by non-parties to the proceeding between Janssen-Cilag and Arrotex Pharmaceuticals. The application was withdrawn in the course of oral argument. The Court&rsquo;s published catchwords record the finding that the non-parties acted unreasonably in commencing and pursuing it.

Two things are worth sitting with.

First, standing to ask is also exposure to pay. A non-party who seeks relief in a proceeding is before the court, and the costs discretion is available in respect of that application.

Second, the court can look backwards. The assessment was not confined to the moment of withdrawal. It reached the decision to commence, and the decision to keep going.

For junior lawyers, the discipline this encourages is a good one. Before you file, ask whether you could justify the application to the bench on the day you abandon it. If the answer is shaky, that is information.

How early in your process do you actually stress test the costs risk of an interlocutory application, rather than treating it as a problem for later?
