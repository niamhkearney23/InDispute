## POST 1, LinkedIn (text post; optionally attach a1.png as the image)

Everyone's favourite magic words are having a rough year in the Federal Court.

In the Greensill insurance litigation, White Oak Commercial Finance is pursuing Insurance Australia Ltd for losses under a trade credit policy written through BCC Trade Credit as IAL's agent. The document battles keep producing privilege rulings, and two of them, months apart, make a matched pair every litigator should read.

Round one: White Oak Commercial Finance Europe (Non-Levered) Ltd v Insurance Australia Ltd (No 3) [2026] FCA 530 (Thawley J, 28 April 2026). White Oak challenged without prejudice privilege claims asserted by BCC and the Tokio Marine parties over settlement-talk documents. The Court held a group of messages did not attract privilege: they canvassed only a general approach the parties might take, with no reference to litigation, no admissions and no observable element of compromise. Talking about a dispute is not negotiating to end it.

Round two: White Oak v Insurance Australia Ltd (Without Prejudice Privilege) [2026] FCA 769, published this week. This time IAL sought production of twelve messages and a draft term sheet White Oak created while negotiating a possible resolution. The Court refused production on a simpler basis: the documents were not directly relevant to any issue in the proceeding, so the privilege question never needed answering.

Three takeaways:

1. Without prejudice privilege follows substance, not headers. A label is not a shield.
2. The asserting party must substantiate the claim with evidence, document by document.
3. Relevance is the first gate. Win there and the privilege fight never starts.

Expect more of these skirmishes as the Greensill fallout grinds on, and expect courts to keep reading the actual messages rather than deferring to the labels on them.

## POST 2, LinkedIn (text post; optionally attach b1.png as the image)

Can a company deal its way out of a Fair Work prosecution? The Full Federal Court just weighed in.

In Elvin v Fair Work Ombudsman [2026] FCAFC 92, the Full Court (Collier, McDonald and Vandongen JJ) reviewed an underpayment case with an unusually rich mix of issues.

The underlying dispute is familiar territory: the Fair Work Ombudsman alleged massage therapists were underpaid, with the case turning in part on whether they were properly classified as Health Professional Level 1 under the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2010. Accessorial liability findings put an individual personally on the hook.

But three features make this one worth your time.

First, procedural fairness. The appellant was self-represented at trial, and the appeal challenged how classic evidentiary rules, Browne v Dunn and Jones v Dunkel, were applied against a litigant without lawyers, alongside bias allegations directed at case management and the conduct of the hearing. Appeal courts take these complaints seriously, and practitioners facing self-represented opponents should too.

Second, the insolvency crossover. The Full Court considered whether the Fair Work Ombudsman is a "creditor" for the purposes of a deed of company arrangement. For directors hoping a DOCA closes the book on workplace claims, this is essential reading, corporate rescue mechanics and regulatory enforcement don't fit together neatly, and personal accessorial liability sits outside the company altogether.

Third, the outcome: appeal allowed in part, cross-appeal dismissed. Neither side walked away clean, which tells you how finely balanced these multi-issue employment appeals can be.

Underpayment enforcement isn't slowing down, and the risk increasingly lands on individuals, not just entities. Classification discipline, fair process and early engagement with the regulator remain the best insurance.
