$750 million is the headline. The more useful story is everything the Court declined to approve.

On 20 November 2026 the Federal Court published Williams v Toyota Motor Corporation Australia Limited (Settlement Approval) [2026] FCA 1781. Lee J approved the class action settlement under s 33V(1) of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth), for a total settlement sum of $750 million inclusive of legal costs and any payment to the litigation funder.

Then the judgment gets interesting. The releases, covenants and other promises purportedly given on behalf of group members were not approved. An order under s 33ZF authorising the applicants nunc pro tunc to enter into and give effect to the deed was refused. Deductions for the applicants&rsquo; legal costs were allowed under s 33V(2), but the quantum of the funder&rsquo;s deduction was reserved. Late registrants were permitted to participate, and a referee is to report on a competitive tender of the settlement administration.

The lesson for anyone learning civil procedure: in a representative proceeding the parties can agree, but they cannot settle. Only the Court can do that, and it can approve the money while refusing the machinery around it.

The number everyone will quote is $750 million. The part worth reading is the refusal.

On 20 November 2026 the Federal Court published Williams v Toyota Motor Corporation Australia Limited (Settlement Approval) [2026] FCA 1781 (Lee J).

What the Court did:

&middot; Approved the proposed class action settlement under s 33V(1) of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth), for a total settlement sum of $750 million, inclusive of legal costs and any payment to the litigation funder.
&middot; Allowed deductions for the applicants&rsquo; legal costs under s 33V(2).
&middot; Permitted late registrants to participate.

What the Court did not do:

&middot; It did not approve the releases, covenants and other promises purportedly given on behalf of group members.
&middot; It refused an order under s 33ZF authorising the applicants nunc pro tunc to enter into and give effect to the deed.
&middot; It reserved the quantum of the funder&rsquo;s deduction.
&middot; It directed that a referee report on a competitive tender of the settlement administration.

For students and juniors, this is a clean illustration of a point that is easy to state and easy to forget in practice: in a representative proceeding the parties negotiate, but only the Court settles. Approval is not a signature at the bottom of a deed. It is a term by term exercise, and the group members who never walked into the courtroom are the people the Court is protecting when it says no.

The competitive tender direction is the detail I would watch. Settlement administration costs come out of the same pot as compensation, and tendering that work is a live question about how much of a settlement fund actually reaches claimants.
