**LinkedIn post:**

The most consequential copyright decision of the year was handed down by a court that declined to decide it.

The facts are almost quaint. In 1962 Cyril Vetter and Donald Smith wrote &ldquo;Double Shot (Of My Baby&rsquo;s Love)&rdquo;. In 1963 the copyright was assigned to Windsong Music Publishers. In 1966 the Swingin&rsquo; Medallions turned it into a hit. In 2019 Vetter served a termination notice under section 304 of the US Copyright Act, effective 2022, and Resnik Music Group acquired Windsong later that year.

The legal question is not quaint at all. Section 304(c)(6)(E) provides that termination affects only rights that arise under the US title and &ldquo;in no way affects rights arising under any other Federal, State, or foreign laws&rdquo;. The orthodox reading, associated with Nimmer, was that a grant of copyright throughout the world is terminable only as to the United States. Vetter argued that the reversion must match the grant: if he gave away the world, he gets the world back.

Judge Shelly Dick agreed in 2025. On 12 January 2026 the Fifth Circuit affirmed in Vetter v Resnik, 163 F.4th 951, Stewart, Circuit Judge, rejecting arguments built on the 1909 Act, on Stewart v Abend, and on territoriality and national treatment under the Berne and Universal Copyright Conventions.

BMG, Sony, Universal and Warner entities acquired the defendant&rsquo;s interest and petitioned for certiorari, warning the ruling would cause chaos across catalogues bought on the opposite assumption.

On 19 October 2026 Justices Sotomayor, Gorsuch, Barrett and Jackson took no part. Five remained. Six are required for a quorum under 28 U.S.C. &sect;1. The qualified Justices concluded the case could not be heard next Term, which triggered 28 U.S.C. &sect;2109: the judgment below stands, with the same effect as affirmance by an equally divided court. No reasons were given for the recusals. Music Business Worldwide reported that BMG&rsquo;s parent, Bertelsmann, also owns Penguin Random House, publisher of books by all four.

For junior lawyers, the transferable lesson is not about music. It is that the value of a decision depends on its status. Vetter has won, completely. But the Fifth Circuit&rsquo;s reasoning binds only Louisiana, Mississippi and Texas, and the Supreme Court has endorsed nothing. Anyone advising on a catalogue purchase now has to price a live split of authority rather than cite a settled rule.

So here is the question I keep returning to: when a reversionary right and a worldwide grant collide, should the reversion be measured by the territory of the statute, or by the scope of what the author actually signed away?
