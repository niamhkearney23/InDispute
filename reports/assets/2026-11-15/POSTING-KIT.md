Hidden cameras in an abattoir. Seven judges of the High Court. One short answer about remedies.

Farm Transparency International Ltd v The Game Meats Company of Australia Pty Ltd [2026] HCA 42 was handed down on 11 November 2026. A director and an employee of the advocacy group trespassed on a halal goat abattoir, installed and retrieved covert cameras, and produced a 14 minute video from the recordings.

The trial judge awarded general and exemplary damages. The Full Federal Court went further and declared that the group held its copyright in the images on constructive trust for the abattoir operator, with injunctions and deletion orders on top.

The High Court unanimously allowed the appeal in part. Damages were inadequate to rectify the consequences of the wrongdoing, but the constructive trust was not needed. What was needed was narrower: orders to delete or destroy all images and copies obtained from the trespasses.

That is the lesson worth carrying into an exam. Inadequacy of damages is the gateway to equitable relief, not a blank cheque. Identify the wrong, identify the consequence, then ask what the least intrusive order is that actually answers it.

Learn this &rarr; Equity grants the minimum relief necessary to address the consequences of the wrong, so a constructive trust will not be imposed where a lesser remedy suffices.

On 11 November 2026 the High Court delivered Farm Transparency International Ltd v The Game Meats Company of Australia Pty Ltd [2026] HCA 42, and it is a clean teaching case on the shape of equitable relief.

The background. Farm Transparency International conducts animal protection advocacy. A director and an employee trespassed on a halal abattoir operated by The Game Meats Company, which slaughters and processes goats for export. They secretly installed covert video cameras, trespassed again to retrieve them, and created a 14 minute video from the recordings.

The litigation. The primary judge in the Federal Court awarded general and exemplary damages for the trespasses, but declined to injunct publication, declined to hold the copyright in the images on constructive trust, and declined to order permanent deletion. The Full Court of the Federal Court allowed an appeal and declared a constructive trust over the copyright, which gave Game Meats a route to a legal assignment and to remedies against third party publishers, together with ancillary injunctions and deletion orders.

The result. A full bench of seven unanimously allowed the appeal in part. Damages were inadequate to rectify the consequences of the wrongdoing. Even so, the only orders needed in equity to address the adverse consequences of the trespasses were orders ensuring that Farm Transparency delete or destroy all images and copies obtained from them. The proprietary remedy fell away.

Why practitioners should care. Clients often ask for the most powerful remedy on the menu. This decision is a reminder that the remedy has to be calibrated to the consequence, and that a proprietary response carries a burden of justification that a narrower order does not. Pleading strategy should start from the harm, not from the remedy you would most like to have.
