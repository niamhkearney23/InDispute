**Instagram caption (copy-paste):**

Safe harbour advice is the most carefully drafted document in a distressed company&rsquo;s file. It is also, on this judgment, one of the easiest to lose.

In <b>McCabe v Mosaic Brands Limited (In Liquidation) (Receivers and Managers Appointed)</b> [2026] FCA 1569, delivered 22 October 2026, Markovic J worked through two questions about advice the Mosaic directors had obtained on the safe harbour provisions of the <i>Corporations Act 2001</i> (Cth).

Question one: whose privilege is it? The directors argued exclusive privilege. The alternative was joint or common interest privilege shared with the Mosaic companies. The Court held the directors had exclusive privilege over the safe harbour advice.

Question two: did they keep it? The advice had been provided to the administrators of the Mosaic companies. It had also been included in Mosaic&rsquo;s board minutes. There was an argument that production had been &ldquo;compelled&rdquo; under the Corporations Act. The Court held the directors waived privilege in the safe harbour advice.

The structure of that result is the lesson. Winning the ownership point and losing the waiver point leaves you in exactly the same place as never holding the privilege at all. Privilege is not a label you attach to a document. It is a state of confidentiality you have to actively maintain, and it is assessed on what you did, not what you meant.

For students: this is a tidy, current authority on waiver by inconsistent conduct, sitting right at the intersection of evidence, corporations and insolvency. Note it.

**LinkedIn post:**

A useful judgment landed on 22 October 2026, and it is one every corporate and restructuring lawyer should read twice.

<b>McCabe v Mosaic Brands Limited, in the matter of Mosaic Brands Limited (In Liquidation) (Receivers and Managers Appointed)</b> [2026] FCA 1569 (Markovic J, Federal Court of Australia).

The subject matter: legal advice obtained by current and former directors of Mosaic Brands Limited and its subsidiaries on the safe harbour provisions of the <i>Corporations Act 2001</i> (Cth).

Two issues.

1. Ownership. Was the safe harbour advice subject to privilege claimed by the directors exclusively, or to joint or common interest privilege shared with the Mosaic companies? The Court held the directors had exclusive privilege over the safe harbour advice.

2. Waiver. Had the directors acted inconsistently with maintaining confidentiality by providing the advice to the administrators of the Mosaic companies? Was that production &ldquo;compelled&rdquo; under the Corporations Act? Did the inclusion of the advice in Mosaic&rsquo;s board minutes amount to waiver? The Court held the directors waived privilege in the safe harbour advice.

Why this is worth your attention.

Safe harbour advice is, by design, advice that gets used. It gets circulated to the board. It gets minuted as the basis for a course of action. If an administrator is later appointed, it gets requested. Every one of those steps is normal, commercially sensible, and governance-positive. This judgment is a reminder that normal and commercially sensible is not the same as privilege-preserving.

The practical takeaway sits in the sequencing. Establishing that the privilege is yours is the easy half. Demonstrating that you have done nothing inconsistent with keeping it confidential is the hard half, and it is decided on conduct rather than intention.

For students and juniors, this is a clean, current illustration of waiver by inconsistency, and a reminder that evidence law does not stay politely inside the evidence exam.

So here is the question I would genuinely like answers to: if safe harbour advice is designed to be acted on, minuted and later explained, can directors realistically preserve privilege in it at all, or should we start treating it as advice that is privileged in theory and disclosed in practice?
