## ⚠️ He cannot be named

Section 534 of the Children, Youth and Families Act prohibits publishing anything identifying a child in these proceedings. The Court itself used randomly chosen initials for exactly that reason.

**Never add:** his name, his suburb, his school, the names of family members, the specific court location, or the TAFE. The slides and captions below carry none of it, and the caption makes the pseudonym part of the story rather than hiding it.

## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

A seventeen year old was sentenced to ten months in detention. On appeal, that was set aside.

He is called RM because the law does not allow him to be named.

The offending was not minor. Stolen cars, weapons, a loaded sawn off shotgun. By the time the appeal was heard he had already served about five months.

Two things about youth sentencing are worth knowing here.

The first is that this kind of appeal is heard completely fresh. The judge sentences again from scratch, rather than hunting for an error in the court below.

The second is a rule most people have never heard of. Under the youth sentencing regime a court must not impose a heavier sentence if a lighter one is appropriate. It is called parsimony, and it is not a mood or a preference. It is a statutory instruction, and it means the lightest adequate sentence is the only lawful one.

What the Court found: early guilty pleas, genuine remorse, and real insight into the drug use and the influence that drove the offending. Complex PTSD. A childhood marked by abandonment and exposure to criminality. Grandparents who have never stopped turning up. A pre-apprenticeship place starting next month.

The result: released, no conviction recorded, a six month supervision order with conditions addressing drugs, mental health and offending. The drug charges were dismissed outright.

His Honour found that more time in custody carried a real risk of undoing the progress he had already made.

Rehabilitation is a purpose of sentencing. It is not the soft option and it is not mercy. It is the thing most likely to prevent the next offence.

*RM v DPP* [2026] VSC 544, Croucher J

## Verification

All from the judgment: aged 17 at the offending and 18 at the appeal; Children's Court total effective sentence of ten months detention; appeal by way of de novo rehearing under ss 424 and 426 of the CYFA; about five months served; drug charges dismissed without conviction; youth supervision order for six months without conviction with special conditions; four month licence disqualification; the parsimony principle in s 361; Bugmy and Verdins considerations; complex PTSD; grandparents' support; pre-apprenticeship at TAFE.

**Left out deliberately:** the assault in custody in which his jaw was broken, and the conditions at the centre. Both are in the judgment and both are real, but on a social post they read as sensational rather than instructive, and neither is needed to make the sentencing point.
