## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Can you keep land you just took?

A laneway is a road. Even a scruffy one behind a pub. If it is a public road, the public has a right to pass along it, and the council has to manage it. It does not stop being a road because it is narrow, or ugly, or because nobody has walked down it in years.

Now, occupying land you do not own is not always fatal. With private land, if you occupy it openly and without permission for long enough, the law can eventually hand you the title. That is adverse possession, or squatter's rights, and it is real.

Against land held by a council or the Crown for public purposes, it generally does not work. Public land is largely carved out of it, for the obvious reason that otherwise anyone could fence off a park and wait.

Here is the twist, though. A council can sell a road.

There is a formal process, called discontinuance, where a council decides a road is no longer needed as a road. Once discontinued, the land can be sold, often to whoever is next door. There is public notice. People get to object.

Which sets up the genuinely hard question.

If someone has occupied a public laneway for years without permission, does that make the sale more likely, or less?

One way: nobody has used it as a road in years, so plainly it is not needed as one. Sell it and tidy up the title.

The other way: it went unused precisely because someone blocked it. Selling it now means the occupation worked.

There is no trick answer. Whether a long standing wrong becomes a reason to change the rule is one of the oldest arguments in law, and you will meet it in property, in equity, and eventually in a council chamber near you.

## Accuracy

Everything on the slides is general principle, stated at a level that holds:

- A public road is land the public is entitled to pass along. **Safe.**
- Adverse possession can give title over private land after long open possession without permission. **Safe.**
- It generally does not run against land held by a council or the Crown for public purposes. **Safe as stated.** The precise statutory exclusions in Victoria sit in the limitations legislation and are more detailed than a slide can carry, which is why the slide says "generally" and names no section.
- Councils can discontinue a road, after public notice, and then sell the land. **Safe.** Again no section cited, deliberately.

**No section numbers anywhere**, because I could not open the legislation from this environment to confirm them, and a wrong section number on a slide is worse than none.

**No names, no business, no dispute.** Slide footers say "General principles of Victorian law. Not about any particular dispute." Keep that line if you edit anything.
