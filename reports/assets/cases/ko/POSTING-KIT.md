## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

A dough mixer was shipped from Toronto to Sydney with 101 packages hidden inside it, about a kilogram each. Canadian authorities found them before it left, took them out, replaced them with an inert substance and let the mixer sail anyway.

So nothing illegal actually arrived. The charge was attempting to import.

Mr Ko worked in sales at a freight forwarder. The prosecution said he used what he knew about logistics to get the consignment through customs and delivered. He said he was helping a friend. A jury convicted him.

The Court of Criminal Appeal quashed that conviction and ordered a new trial. The Crown appealed to the High Court. Last week the Crown lost, five judges to two.

The whole case is one distinction, and it is a distinction worth carrying into an exam.

Awareness of a risk: there is a real chance there is something in this box.

Intention: even if there is something in this box, I am prepared to bring it in.

The first is not enough. The second is intention. And the jury here was told that if it found Mr Ko saw a real chance of a substance in the mixer, it was open to infer that he intended to import it. It was never told that it still had to go on and decide whether he actually meant to.

That gap was the miscarriage. Not proof the verdict was wrong. Proof the process was.

Edelman J put the danger in one line. On a purely literal reading of what the High Court had said before, he wrote, it might have been suggested that people could be imprisoned for stupidity in Australia now.

Because that is who the line protects. Not the careful criminal. The person who was careless rather than criminal, who ran a risk they should not have run, and who a loose direction convicts of meaning something they never meant.

Two judges disagreed and would have upheld the conviction, reading the directions as a whole and in the context of the trial that was actually run.

Mr Ko now faces a retrial. Nothing has been decided against him.

## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; attach slide 4)

The King v Ko [2026] HCA 29 tidies up something that has been quietly troubling since Smith and Afford, and it does it by stating three propositions plainly.

First, it is not sufficient, in proving that an accused intended to import a substance, to establish only that the accused was aware of, or perceived, a real or significant chance that they were importing a substance.

Second, it is sufficient to establish that the accused was aware of such a chance and that their state of mind was: even if the substance is in the object, I am prepared to take the object into Australia.

Third, a miscarriage may be occasioned if the directions leave a real risk that the jury will treat the first as satisfying the element.

The trial judge here gave a direction squarely within the Smith and Afford propositions: if satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the accused perceived a real or substantial chance of a substance being present, it was open to infer intent. What was absent was the further instruction that, even so, the jury had to go on and consider whether it was proved that he meant to import the substance.

Two features made that absence material. The summary of the competing cases was framed in terms capable of suggesting that the Crown case on the intent element was made out on perception of risk alone. And, unlike Kural, Smith and Afford, this accused did not personally carry anything into the country, so the reasoning in Smith and Afford about the improbability of a state of mind short of intent, which rests on the opportunity to inspect or declare, simply did not apply.

Edelman J went further, and practitioners should note it: the expression "real or significant chance", and its variants, should usually be avoided in a direction on intention altogether, outside conditional intention cases. Jagot J made the related point that proposition (8)(ii) of Smith and Afford cannot be applied in the terms in which it is expressed and in isolation from its context.

Steward J and Gleeson J dissented. Gleeson J's reasons repay reading on the distinction between a direction and a summary of a party's case, and on the discipline of assessing a summing up against the trial that was actually had.

The conviction stands quashed and the matter is remitted for retrial.
