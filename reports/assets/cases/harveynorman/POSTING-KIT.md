## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Sixty months interest free. No deposit. You have seen the ad, probably hundreds of times.

Last month the Federal Court set the price of it at $55 million.

Here is what was actually being advertised. Between January 2020 and August 2021, Harvey Norman and Latitude Finance ran a national campaign in newspapers, on radio and on television, thousands of times, promoting home and electrical goods on sixty equal monthly repayments, no deposit, interest free.

All of that was true.

What the ads did not say is that to get it you had to take out a credit card. And that the card carried an establishment fee and a monthly account service fee. A customer who signed up in the relevant window, bought on the sixty month plan and paid it off over the full sixty months would be liable for at least $537 in fees on top of the purchase price.

Interest free. Not free.

ASIC sued in 2022 under sections 12DA, 12DB and 12DF of the ASIC Act. In October 2024, Yates J found both companies had engaged in misleading conduct and made false or misleading representations. Both sought leave to appeal. In September 2025 the Full Court granted leave and dismissed the appeals, describing the applications as unmeritorious and saying it was regrettable that the determination of remedies had been delayed by them.

Then, last month, the penalties. $35 million against Harvey Norman. $20 million against Latitude. Both companies ordered to run corrective advertising on their website home pages for ninety days.

Harvey Norman's share was increased because of statements made by its chair, which the Court said showed a disregard for the potential harm suffered by consumers from the misleading conduct. Worth sitting with, that one. What a company says publicly about a finding against it is itself relevant to what the finding costs.

The legal point is the one worth carrying.

Misleading conduct is not about whether each sentence is accurate. It is about the impression the whole thing leaves on an ordinary person seeing it. Every element of this offer was true in isolation. Read together, in a fifteen second television spot, they conveyed something that was not.

Which is why "but it was in the terms and conditions" has never been a defence, and why the omission is doing the work here rather than any false statement.

If you are studying consumer law or contract, this is your worked example for the semester. It has a national campaign, a four year fight, a clean statutory basis, and a number attached.

## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; attach slide 3)

A useful decision for anyone who signs off on financial services advertising.

On 28 July the Federal Court imposed penalties of $35 million against Harvey Norman Holdings Ltd and $20 million against Latitude Finance Australia over a national campaign promoting a sixty month interest free, no deposit payment method for goods bought at Harvey Norman stores. The campaign ran in newspapers, on television and on radio between January 2020 and August 2021.

Three things are worth drawing out.

First, the contravention was one of omission, not falsity. Each component of the offer was accurate. What the advertisements did not convey was that the payment method required the consumer to take out a credit card, and that the card attracted an establishment fee and ongoing monthly account service fees. A consumer who took up the offer in the relevant period and repaid over the full term stood to incur at least $537 in fees. ASIC pleaded ss 12DA, 12DB and 12DF of the ASIC Act, and Yates J found the conduct misleading and the representations false or misleading in October 2024.

Second, the procedural history is its own lesson in cost. Both respondents sought leave to appeal. The Full Court granted leave and dismissed the appeals in September 2025, observing that it was regrettable that the final determination of remedies had been delayed by what it described as unmeritorious applications. Four years elapsed between commencement and penalty.

Third, and most practically for boards: Harvey Norman's penalty was increased by reference to statements made by its chair, which the Court considered showed a disregard for the potential harm suffered by consumers. Public commentary on an adverse finding is not costless. It goes to contrition, and contrition goes to the penalty.

The Court also ordered corrective advertising on both companies' website home pages for ninety days.

For anyone reviewing campaign creative: the question is not whether each claim can be substantiated. It is what the overall impression conveys to the ordinary and reasonable member of the audience, and whether the material terms that qualify the headline offer are conveyed with it.

That sequence is possible but I could not verify it against the judgment itself from this environment, so no penalty judge is named anywhere on the slides.
