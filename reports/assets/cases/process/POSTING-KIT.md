## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Three High Court appeals this month. Not one of them was decided on the question it appeared to be about.

**Farrugia v The King.** It looked like a case about whether one barrister can represent two co-offenders at the same sentencing hearing, when their interests point in opposite directions. It was decided on the fact that he filed no evidence of what he was advised or what he instructed. The Court said that had the conflict been proved, it would have mattered. He could not prove it, so his sentence stands.

**The King v Ko.** It looked like a case about whether a man meant to import 101 packages hidden inside a commercial dough mixer. It was decided on one sentence a judge did not say to the jury: that even if they found he saw a real chance of a substance being in there, they still had to go on and decide whether he actually meant to import it. Conviction quashed. New trial.

**Plaintiff S32/2026 v Minister.** It looked like a case about whether a man would be safe if he were returned to Nepal. It was decided on which court he walked into first. There was a rung on the review ladder he never used, and going over the top of it was an abuse of process.

Here is the thing worth taking out of that.

Law school grades you on the law. It sets a problem, gives you the facts, and asks what the answer is. That is a useful skill and it is not the one that decided any of these.

Courts decide on the record. On what was filed and what was not. On what someone conceded out loud at a hearing three years ago. On whether you went to the right court in the right order inside the right window.

Evidence and civil procedure get taught as the subjects you get through. They are the subjects that decided all three of these, and every one of them has a version where the other side wins without anybody knowing more law.

If you are picking electives, that is the argument for taking the dry ones seriously. And if you are sitting in a courtroom over summer watching real matters run, that is the thing to actually watch. Not the submissions. The paperwork.

## LinkedIn version (copy-paste; attach slide 1)

Three High Court appeals delivered this month, and a pattern worth naming for anyone teaching or supervising junior lawyers.

In Farrugia v The King [2026] HCA 28, the appellant alleged his Senior Counsel had been placed in a position of conflict by appearing for him and a co-offender at the same sentencing hearing. The appeal was dismissed because he adduced no evidence of the advice he received or the instructions he gave, and maintained privilege over those communications. The Court observed that had the irregularity been established, it would have been material.

In The King v Ko [2026] HCA 29, the Crown's appeal was dismissed and a conviction for attempting to import a commercial quantity of border controlled drugs remains quashed. The defect was the absence of a direction that, even on a finding that the accused perceived a real or significant chance of a substance being present, the jury still had to be satisfied that he meant to import it.

In Plaintiff S32/2026 v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship [2026] HCA 27, an application for constitutional writs was dismissed with costs as an abuse of process, judicial review of the Tribunal's confirmation decision having been available in the FCFCA and never sought.

One turned on the evidentiary burden. One turned on the adequacy of a direction. One turned on the order in which remedies are pursued. None turned on a contested proposition of substantive law.

That is not a criticism of any of them, and it is not unusual. It is simply what appellate work mostly is, and it is worth saying to students, who are assessed almost entirely on the substantive question and almost never on the record.
