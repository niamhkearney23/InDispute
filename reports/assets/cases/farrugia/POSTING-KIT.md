## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

One barrister appeared for two co-offenders at the same sentencing hearing. He then told the judge that his own client was the more culpable of the two. His client got the longer sentence.

Last week the High Court held that was not enough to overturn it.

Frank Farrugia and Deniz Kanmaz both pleaded guilty to conspiring to traffic a commercial quantity of controlled drugs. The same Senior Counsel appeared for both in the District Court of New South Wales. There is no rule against that.

The Crown filed written submissions saying Kanmaz should receive the more substantial penalty. More drugs, more transactions, a larger money laundering charge, a longer criminal record.

Senior Counsel did not adopt that. He argued the opposite.

The judge checked, twice. You agree, though, that Mr Kanmaz is more culpable than Mr Farrugia, don't you? No, I say the opposite. The Crown then conceded he was right about that.

Farrugia was sentenced to 11 years, non parole seven and a half. Kanmaz got nine years, non parole six and a half.

On appeal Farrugia argued his counsel had been placed in a position of conflict between his duty to one client and his duty to the other, and that he should have been separately represented.

The appeal was dismissed.

The reason is the part worth learning. Farrugia never put on any evidence of what he was advised or what he instructed. He maintained legal professional privilege over those communications, which he was entitled to do. But whether counsel is conflicted is assessed by looking at the client's interests, and a client's instructions bear on where those interests lie. Without them the Court said the analysis could not be taken further. The most that could be said was that a conflict was possible.

He carried the onus of proving the error and he did not discharge it.

The Court also made clear this was not a technicality. It set the threshold for when an irregularity vitiates a sentence: it must be something that could realistically have affected the sentencing judge's reasoning. Had the conflict been established, that threshold would have been met.

Two judges would have allowed the appeal. Edelman J held there was an actual conflict the moment the Crown's submissions landed, because Senior Counsel then had to choose which client to disappoint. Jagot J held the conflict was irreconcilable and that no client could consent to their own interests being subordinated to a co-accused's.

There is no rule stopping counsel from appearing for two co-offenders. After this, there is a very good reason not to.

## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; attach slide 1)

Two things come out of Farrugia v The King [2026] HCA 28, delivered on 12 August, and the second is the one that will be cited.

The first is the materiality threshold on a sentence appeal. Adapting Brawn to sentencing, the plurality held that a specific error or procedural irregularity vitiates the exercise of the sentencing discretion where it could realistically have affected the reasoning of the sentencing judge to the sentence imposed. Kentwell contemplates a distinction between material and non material specific errors, and the NSW DPP's submission that no materiality threshold applies to specific error was rejected.

The second is evidentiary, and it is where the appeal was actually lost.

Senior Counsel appeared for the appellant and a co-offender at a joint sentencing hearing. Contrary to the Crown's written submissions, he contended that the appellant's offending was the more objectively serious of the two. The Crown conceded the point orally. The appellant received 11 years with a non parole period of seven and a half; the co-offender nine years with six and a half.

On appeal the appellant contended Senior Counsel was in a position of conflict. He adduced no evidence of the advice he received or the instructions he gave, and maintained privilege over those communications.

The Court held that whether a barrister is in a position of conflict is assessed objectively, but by reference to the client's interests, and the client's instructions bear upon where those interests lie. Citing Mills v The Queen, resolution of a conflicting duties complaint will almost invariably require the court to be apprised of the client's instructions. On the record here, the contents of the agreed facts, the course of the hearing and the concessions made below did not reveal a conflict so overt as to render advice and instructions irrelevant. The most that could be said was that a conflict was possible. The persuasive and evidentiary burden was the appellant's and it was not discharged.

Two points of practical significance. Had the irregularity been established, the Court said it would have been material. And the Court noted that the concessions made in the Court of Criminal Appeal about the advice given and the appellant's acquiescence most likely effected a waiver over the very communications privilege was being asserted over.

Edelman J and Jagot J would have allowed the appeal. Edelman J distinguished apparent, potential and actual conflict, holding that the obligations in rr 35 and 119 of the Barristers Rules are imposed by law and are not exhausted by what a client instructs. Jagot J went further: counsel can never obtain, and a client can never give, informed consent to counsel subordinating that client's best interests to a co-accused's.

The practice remains permissible. It is now considerably harder to defend.
