## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

He had a removal date. He went straight to the High Court. The High Court said that was an abuse of process.

Here is the sequence, because the sequence is the case.

A delegate refused his protection visa. Notably, the delegate accepted he would suffer significant harm if returned. The refusal turned on a different question: whether the authorities in Nepal could protect him from it. The delegate found they could.

He asked the Administrative Review Tribunal to review that on the merits. His hearing was listed. He did not appear. The Tribunal dismissed the application, then refused to reinstate it.

Then he was issued a notice of intention to remove him from Australia.

Days out, he applied to the High Court for constitutional writs, orders under section 75(v) of the Constitution directed at the Minister. The High Court restrained his removal while it heard him.

Then it dismissed the application, with costs.

The reason is the useful part. There was a rung on the ladder he never used. He could have asked the Federal Circuit and Family Court to judicially review the Tribunal's refusal to reinstate. Win there and the Tribunal has to look at the merits again. He never asked.

He aimed at the visa decision instead, which that court has no jurisdiction over, because it is a primary decision.

The Court's words: the plaintiff should not be permitted to circumvent the hierarchy of review provided for by the Migration Act. Applications progress through the hierarchy of tribunals and courts, usually ending in the High Court only after special leave. Not by starting there.

It was also out of time. The window is 35 days. Extending it depends on the length of the delay, the reasons for it, and whether the case has merit. On merit the Court went on and found no jurisdictional error anyway.

For students, the thing to take out of this is not the outcome. It is that the hierarchy you learn as background in administrative law is not background. Here it was the answer, and an imminent removal date did not excuse skipping it.

## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; attach slide 3)

A short but useful decision on the limits of the s 75(v) jurisdiction as a practical remedy.

In Plaintiff S32/2026 v Minister for Immigration and Citizenship [2026] HCA 27, delivered on 5 August, Gordon, Steward and Beech-Jones JJ dismissed with costs an application for constitutional writs directed at a protection visa refusal.

The plaintiff's review application had been dismissed by the Administrative Review Tribunal under s 99 of the ART Act for non-appearance, and the Tribunal had confirmed that dismissal on a reinstatement application under s 102. He did not challenge either of those decisions. Faced with a notice of intention to remove, he applied to the High Court in respect of the visa decision itself.

The Court held the proceeding an abuse of process. Judicial review of the confirmation decision was available in the FCFCA, subject to an extension of time under s 477(2), and success there would have carried with it a further opportunity for merits review of the visa decision on remitter. The FCFCA has no jurisdiction over the visa decision itself, it being a primary decision within s 476(2)(a), and there was no power to remit to the Federal Court. The plaintiff should not be permitted to circumvent the hierarchy of review the Migration Act provides.

Two points on the merits, addressed in any event.

On the mandatory consideration ground, the delegate had referred to the 2024 DFAT report for the purpose of the s 36(2B)(b) finding. That the report was not cited under the particular headings the plaintiff attacked did not mean it was overlooked, and there was no contention that the delegate was precluded from also considering the 2019 report.

On illogicality, the Court rejected the premise. The statement about the relative effectiveness of urban policing was not the critical consideration. What was critical were the plaintiff's personal circumstances, in particular that the threat was tied to his ability and expressed willingness to repay, and that there was no information suggesting the authorities would withhold protection or decline to investigate.

Extension of time refused: the application was an abuse of process and disclosed no jurisdictional error.
