## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

A former solicitor is going to prison for seven days. He is 88 years old. The dispute was about a password.

The Victorian Legal Services Board had appointed a manager to run his law practice. That happens when a practitioner is under investigation, so that clients are not left stranded with files nobody can reach.

The manager needed access to the email account the practice had been run from. He would not hand it over.

A judge ordered him to. He did not comply. A second judge made the order again, in substantially the same terms, this time with a warning attached that failure could result in imprisonment. He still did not comply. He was found guilty of contempt in June. He sought leave to appeal, and the Court of Appeal refused it in July.

Then came the penalty hearing.

His own IT consultant was called to give evidence. Shown the order for the first time in the witness box, he agreed that compliance was straightforward, and that if authorised he could provide the password in about ten minutes.

The password was handed over that afternoon, after the adjournment.

His Honour recorded a conviction, imposed 45 days imprisonment, and suspended all but seven of them.

Three things in the reasoning are worth carrying with you.

The first is that an appeal does not pause an order. The obligation to obey subsists until a court sets the order aside. Disagreeing with an order, even reasonably, is not a defence to disobeying it, and this is not a technicality: it is the difference between a legal system and a negotiation.

The second is the distinction between civil and criminal contempt. Disobeying an order is usually civil, and the point of the sanction is to force compliance. It becomes criminal when the defiance is deliberate. The word the courts use is contumacious, and the finding here was that the refusal had been calculating, deliberate, wilful and contumacious.

The third is the one that should stay with any law student reading this. His age and his decades in practice counted against him, not for him. The court is entitled to expect more of someone admitted as an officer of the court, because that person of all people understands exactly what an order is. Deliberate disobedience by a lawyer is treated as an attack on the court's authority by someone who makes their living from it.

He had ceased practising and is an undischarged bankrupt, so a fine would have meant nothing and a wholly suspended sentence would have meant very little. That is why seven days are to be served.

## Shorter caption, if you want it tighter

A former solicitor is going to prison for seven days, over a password.

A manager had been appointed to run his practice while he was under investigation. The manager needed the email account the practice ran on. He refused to hand over the password. A judge ordered him to. He refused. A second judge ordered him again, with a warning that prison was possible. He still refused, and the Court of Appeal turned him away.

At the penalty hearing his own IT expert said compliance would take ten minutes. He handed it over that afternoon.

Conviction recorded. 45 days, seven to serve. He is 88.

The principle is old and unforgiving: an appeal does not pause an order. Until a court sets it aside, you obey it. And the court expects more of a lawyer, not less, because an officer of the court knows exactly what an order is.
