## The five, and why each earned its place

| Case | The point | Why this one and not another |
|---|---|---|
| **Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd** [1897] AC 22 | Separate legal personality | The foundation of the entire law of corporations. Nothing else competes. |
| **Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co** [1893] 1 QB 256 | Offer to the world, acceptance by performance | The most famous contract case in the common law, and genuinely funny, which matters on a feed. |
| **Masters v Cameron** (1954) 91 CLR 353 | The three categories of "subject to contract" | Australian, High Court, and the one students most often cannot state cleanly under exam pressure. |
| **Waltons Stores v Maher** (1988) 164 CLR 387 | Promissory estoppel as a cause of action | The moment Australian law departed from England. High Court pride, and a great fact pattern. |
| **Codelfa v State Rail Authority** (1982) 149 CLR 337 | The gateway to extrinsic evidence in construction | Still actively fought over, so it connects the canon to live practice. |

**Deliberately not included:** Donoghue v Stevenson. It is the most famous case of all and it is tort, not commercial. The account already has a whole negligence deck for it.

## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Five commercial cases you will be expected to know. Not the newest ones. The ones that keep turning up, in exams and in practice, a century after they were decided.

**Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd** (1897). Mr Salomon turned his boot business into a company, took security over it, and when it collapsed his creditors argued the company was really just him wearing a hat. The House of Lords said no. A company is a legal person separate from the people who own it. Everything you will ever learn about corporate structure, limited liability and piercing the veil starts here.

**Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co** (1893). A company advertised that its smoke ball prevented influenza, and offered £100 to anyone who used it properly and got sick anyway. To prove it was serious it said it had deposited £1,000 at a bank. Mrs Carlill used the ball, caught influenza, and sued. She won. An offer can be made to the world at large, and accepted by performance, without telling the offeror first.

**Masters v Cameron** (1954). People shake hands on terms and then write "subject to contract". Are they bound? The High Court gave three categories. Bound immediately, with the formal document just tidying it up. Bound, but performance waits for the document. Or not bound at all until it is signed. If you can state those three cleanly you are ahead of most of your cohort.

**Waltons Stores v Maher** (1988). The Mahers demolished their building and started construction because Waltons had led them to believe the lease was going ahead. Waltons had not signed, and then went quiet, watching. There was no contract. The High Court held Waltons was estopped from denying one anyway, which turned promissory estoppel from a shield into a sword. This is where Australian law walked away from the English position.

**Codelfa v State Rail Authority** (1982). A tunnelling contractor priced its work assuming three shifts a day. An injunction stopped night work. The contract said nothing about shifts. When can you look outside the words of an agreement to work out what it means? Australian courts are still arguing about the answer, which is exactly why you need to know the question.

Here is the thing about old cases.

They are not history. Every one of these is doing work in a commercial dispute somewhere in Australia this week, being cited in submissions by someone who is billing for it. That is why they are examinable, and it is why knowing five of them cold is worth more than knowing last month's judgment.

Save this one for exam block.

## LinkedIn version (copy-paste; attach slide 1)

A question worth putting to anyone supervising law students this year: could your junior state the three categories in Masters v Cameron without looking them up?

Five cases carry a disproportionate amount of Australian commercial practice, and none of them is recent.

Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22 for separate legal personality. Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893] 1 QB 256 for offers to the world and acceptance by conduct. Masters v Cameron (1954) 91 CLR 353 for whether a document expressed to be subject to contract binds. Waltons Stores (Interstate) Ltd v Maher (1988) 164 CLR 387 for promissory estoppel as a cause of action. Codelfa Construction Pty Ltd v State Rail Authority of NSW (1982) 149 CLR 337 for the admissibility of surrounding circumstances in construing a contract.

Three of the five are High Court decisions and two of those, Waltons Stores and Codelfa, are places where Australian law deliberately diverged or declined to follow the English line. That divergence is itself worth teaching.

The practical case for drilling the canon rather than the current list is simple. A recent judgment tells a junior what the law is on one point today. These five tell them how the arguments are built, which transfers.

## Verification

No verification table is needed in the usual sense. There are no current proceedings, no living parties, no outcomes that could be overtaken, and nothing here that could be wrong about a real person. Every proposition is the settled ratio of a decision that has been in the standard texts for decades.

**Two points of care, both handled:**

- **Codelfa is stated as a question, not an answer.** The slide and the caption say Australian courts are still arguing about when extrinsic evidence is admissible. That is accurate and it is why the case is worth knowing. The post does not assert what the current position is, because that is genuinely contested.
- **Waltons Stores is described as estoppel, not contract.** The slide says explicitly that there was no contract. That distinction is the entire point of the case and it is the thing students most often blur.

**Before reposting in a later semester:** nothing here goes stale, but check that Codelfa has not been displaced by a subsequent High Court decision on surrounding circumstances. That is the only slide with any exposure to change at all.
