**LinkedIn post:**

When two related companies collapse together, which one actually employed the workers, and does it matter? In a group insolvency, it can decide who gets paid first.

Strandline Resources and its subsidiary Coburn Resources ran a mineral sands project together. Both entered receivership and voluntary administration on the same day, with Robert Brauer, Jason Preston and Linda Smith of McGrathNicol appointed. 166 workers had written contracts naming Strandline, the parent, as their employer, even though the work was performed on Coburn's project.

The employer's identity mattered because unpaid employee entitlements carry priority in an insolvency. The Commonwealth, having paid those entitlements through the fair entitlements guarantee scheme and standing in the workers' shoes by subrogation, wanted that priority claim to attach to Coburn, where more asset value sat, ahead of Coburn's secured lenders.

Justice Jackson of the Federal Court held the written contracts naming Strandline as employer ordinarily prevail. An "intelligible business objective" for how a corporate group structures employment is a relevant consideration, but it is not a standalone test that can override a clear, formal contract.

The practical effect: the Commonwealth's priority claim attaches to Strandline, not Coburn, and Coburn's secured lenders are not leapfrogged.

For anyone advising on corporate group structures, the lesson is a simple one with real consequences: get the employment contracts right, because an insolvency will test exactly who they name, and formal documents will usually win over an argument about where the work actually happened.
