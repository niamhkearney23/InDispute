## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Nobody reads all 470 paragraphs. Here is how to actually read a judgment.

**One. Start at the end.** Go straight to the orders. Dismissed, allowed, set aside, sent back. Everything else in the judgment exists to explain that one line, so read the answer before you read the working.

**Two. Read the catchwords.** That block at the top between the dashes, the one everyone scrolls past. It lists the area of law, the issue, the sections and the key authorities. It is the judgment summarising itself, written by someone who read the whole thing.

**Three. Find the questions.** Judges almost always name the issues in the first few paragraphs. Usually two or three questions decide the entire case. Write them down before you read another word, or you will read 200 paragraphs without knowing what you are looking for.

**Four. Read only what answers them.** You can skip most of the middle. Use the section headings, find the ones matching your questions, read those properly and skim the rest.

**Five. Know who is talking.** This is the one that catches people.

"The Bank submits" is not a finding. "The plaintiff contends" is not a finding. Those paragraphs are the court setting out an argument it may be about to reject.

What you want is: I find. I am satisfied. I accept. I reject. In my view. That is the judge speaking. Everything else is somebody's case.

Get that wrong and you will confidently write down the losing party's argument as the ratio. It happens constantly, including to people who should know better.

Ten minutes done this way gets you the case. An hour gets you the detail. Do the ten minutes first, and the hour will be far more useful.

## LinkedIn version (copy-paste; attach slide 1)

The most common mistake I see in case notes is not misunderstanding the law. It is misattributing it.

A judgment sets out each party's submissions at length before the court says what it thinks. Those passages read exactly like reasoning, because they are reasoning. They are just somebody else's, and often the losing side's.

"The respondent submits" is not a holding. "It was contended that" is not a holding. The holding is in the paragraphs beginning I find, I am satisfied, I accept, I reject.

For students starting a case note, the sequence that saves the most time is simple. Read the orders first, so you know the destination. Read the catchwords second, because they are the judgment's own summary of what it is about. Identify the two or three questions the court says it has to answer. Then read only the sections that answer them, and check the speaker of every proposition you intend to quote.

Ten minutes spent that way makes the following hour substantially more useful.
