## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

A partner sued his own firm for age discrimination. The firm tried to have the case thrown out before it could be heard. Last week it failed.

Start with the facts, because they matter.

HWL Ebsworth was planning to float. In August 2020 the then managing partner told Gregory Lewis he would be excluded from the proposed listing, citing his history of contribution, performance and overdrawing. Lewis was 66. The email did not mention his age. In November 2020 the partners resolved to expel him. The float never went ahead.

Lewis sued in New South Wales, but that case was about the partnership deed and fiduciary duty, not discrimination. He won at first instance on a narrow point, then lost on appeal with costs, and the High Court refused special leave.

His age came up in argument along the way. It was never the claim.

So in October 2025 he started a fresh proceeding in Victoria, this time as an age discrimination claim under the Equal Opportunity Act 2010 (Vic).

HWL Ebsworth said that was an abuse of process. One dispute, one set of facts, one bite at it. He had his chance and he should have run everything the first time.

The Court disagreed, and the reasoning is the useful part.

No cause of action estoppel, because the New South Wales Supreme Court and the High Court have no original jurisdiction to hear a claim under a Victorian statute. No issue estoppel, because no court had ever decided whether he was treated less favourably because of his age, so he never had a fair and full opportunity to run it. No Anshun estoppel, because the discrimination claim was not so connected with the earlier case that it was unreasonable not to have brought it there.

And not an abuse of process. Her Honour accepted that Lewis did not appreciate that his age might be the reason until the Court of Appeal judgment, and rejected the argument that he should have started in a federal commission and then tried to cross-vest the claim into the NSW proceedings. That route, she said, had an air of unreality.

Now the important part.

Nothing has been decided about whether any discrimination happened. This ruling decided one thing: the case can be heard. That is it.

What it is really about is the tension every civil procedure course is built on. Finality says litigation has to end. Access to justice says a claim nobody has ever adjudicated deserves a hearing. Here, access won, because the earlier court could not have heard the claim anyway.

## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; attach slide 1)

A useful ruling for anyone who advises on the reach of Anshun estoppel.

In Lewis v HWL Ebsworth Lawyers [2026] VSC 514, delivered on 12 August, Ierodiaconou AsJ refused the firm's application to strike out, summarily dismiss or permanently stay a former partner's age discrimination claim brought under the Equal Opportunity Act 2010 (Vic).

The background is that Mr Lewis had already litigated his exclusion from a proposed IPO and his expulsion from the partnership in New South Wales, as a partnership deed and fiduciary duty case. He was unsuccessful on appeal and special leave was refused. Age was raised in argument in those proceedings, principally in response to submissions that he was in the twilight of his career, but discrimination was never pleaded.

Four points from the reasons.

First, jurisdiction defeats the estoppel argument at the threshold. The Supreme Court of New South Wales and the High Court have no original jurisdiction over a complaint under a Victorian statute, so there was no cause of action estoppel and no res judicata.

Second, on issue estoppel, applying Tomlinson v Ramsey Food Processing, no court had determined whether the plaintiff was treated less favourably because of his age. He had not had a fair and full opportunity to present that case, so nothing was concluded against him.

Third, on Anshun, her Honour found the discrimination claim was not so connected with the subject matter of the earlier proceedings that it was unreasonable not to have raised it, noting that the plaintiff relies on rather than contests the Court of Appeal's findings, so there is no risk of inconsistent fact finding.

Fourth, and practically, the Court rejected the submission that the plaintiff should have commenced in a human rights commission and then sought referral and cross-vesting into the NSW proceedings. That course was described as having an air of unreality, citing Meriton Apartments for the proposition that it is not unreasonable to decline to litigate in a forum whose jurisdiction may be open to challenge.

Delay was addressed rather than ignored. Five years had passed and a key witness has died, but the firm did not contend that a fair trial was impossible, and much of the delay was explained by when the plaintiff came to appreciate that age might have been the reason.

To state the obvious, because it will otherwise be misread: this is an interlocutory ruling. No finding has been made that any discrimination occurred.
