On 4 November 2026 the High Court dismissed Uber Australia&rsquo;s appeal against a payroll tax assessment of about $81 million.

The structure looked tidy. Drivers contracted with Uber. Riders contracted with drivers through the apps. Uber collected the fares and passed them on. On Uber&rsquo;s case it was never supplied with the drivers&rsquo; services at all, so the contractor provisions in the Payroll Tax Act 2007 (NSW) could not reach it.

**LinkedIn post:**

Uber Australia Pty Ltd v Chief Commissioner of State Revenue [2026] HCA 40 was handed down on 4 November 2026, and it is the gig economy decision commercial lawyers will be citing for years.

The facts are deceptively simple. Uber operated a digital rideshare system. Drivers entered a contract with Uber. Riders entered a separate contract with the driver through the Uber applications. Uber collected fares from riders and remitted them to drivers. The Chief Commissioner of State Revenue assessed Uber for payroll tax on those remitted fares, approximately $81 million for the financial years ending 30 June 2015 to 30 June 2020.

The battleground was Division 7 of Part 3 of the Payroll Tax Act 2007 (NSW), which extends payroll tax beyond employment to relevant contracts. Were the Driver Contracts relevant contracts under s 32? Were the driving services merely ancillary to the use of goods? Were the remitted fares deemed wages under s 35?

The High Court dismissed the appeal with costs and upheld the New South Wales Court of Appeal. Uber was an employer liable for payroll tax on so much of the money it collected from riders and paid to drivers as the Chief Commissioner determined was attributable to the performance of work by those drivers. As Gageler CJ put it, the conclusion reached by the Court of Appeal was correct.

The practical takeaway is not really about rideshare. It is that intermediary structures are read for the rights and obligations they create, not for the commercial labels placed on them. Payment collection agent is a description, not a defence.

So here is the question for the platform economy: how many intermediary businesses have genuinely stress tested their contracts against s 32, rather than against their own org chart?
