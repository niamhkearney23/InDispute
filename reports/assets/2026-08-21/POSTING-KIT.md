## ⚠️ Accuracy notes, read before posting

**Marriott is an interlocutory ruling.** The assault is **alleged**. Nothing has been decided about whether it happened, and the claim against the first defendant has not been proved, it has merely survived a strike out and must now be re-pleaded. Slide 5 says "says she was assaulted" for that reason. Do not let a caption or a reply turn that into a finding.

**Guss and Bogojevska name real people who have been convicted or sentenced.** Both are accurate as stated and both come straight from the judgments. Keep any replies to what the judgment says.

**RM is a child offender under a pseudonym.** Section 534 of the Children, Youth and Families Act prohibits identifying him. The slide uses only the initials the Court itself used, and no identifying detail. Do not add any.

**Bogojevska: the victim is named** in the judgment, but the slide does not name her. That was deliberate. Naming a deceased victim on a social post adds nothing and risks distressing family, and the judgment records that her son died days before the plea hearing.

## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Eight judgments out of the Supreme Court of Victoria in three days. Four worth knowing about.

**Victorian Legal Services Board v Guss** [2026] VSC 529. A former solicitor was ordered to hand the regulator's appointed manager the password to the email account he ran his law practice from. He refused, through two judges and a failed appeal, arguing the order should never have been made. At the penalty hearing his own IT consultant gave evidence it would take ten minutes. He handed it over that afternoon. Conviction recorded, 45 days imprisonment, seven to be served. He is 88.

The principle is old and unforgiving: an appeal does not stay an order. Until it is set aside, you obey it.

**DPP v Bogojevska** [2026] VSC 534. A guilty plea to the manslaughter of an 85 year old neighbour, by an unlawful and dangerous act. What lifted the sentence was everything that came after: the body hidden for a day, theft from the deceased, her bank card used, and her body left by a river. Eight years, six non-parole. But for the plea, nine.

**RM v DPP** [2026] VSC 544. A seventeen year old was sentenced to ten months detention in the Children's Court. On appeal, heard fresh, that was set aside. Five months already served, a supervision order for six months, no conviction, and a pre-apprenticeship at TAFE starting next month. The drug charges were dismissed outright.

Under the youth sentencing regime, a court cannot reach for a heavier order if a lighter one will do. That is the parsimony principle, and this is what it looks like applied.

**Marriott v Grigorovitch** [2026] VSC 535. A woman says she was assaulted at a political party branch meeting and sued two fellow members for failing to protect her. One claim was summarily dismissed. The other survives, though the pleading has to be redone. Being members of the same association creates no duty of care on its own.

Also that week: an expert report refused because it arrived mid-trial, an adjournment refusal held not to be unfair, a costs order split down the middle, and a set of class action writs kept alive another year.

Half of the eight turned on process rather than substance. That is not a coincidence, and it is worth remembering the next time a deadline looks negotiable.

## LinkedIn version (copy-paste; attach slide 2)

Three judgments from a single week in the Supreme Court of Victoria, each a reminder that process is not the soft part of practice.

In Victorian Legal Services Board v Guss (Penalty) [2026] VSC 529, Finanzio J recorded a conviction and imposed 45 days imprisonment, seven to be served, on a former solicitor for contempt. The order breached was straightforward: provide the manager appointed under the Uniform Law with the password to the email account used to conduct the practice. His Honour found the refusal calculating, deliberate and contumacious, and therefore criminal rather than civil in character. The contempt was purged only at the penalty hearing, after the contemnor's own IT consultant gave evidence that compliance would have taken ten minutes.

Two points of general application. First, an appeal does not operate as a stay; the obligation to comply subsists until the order is discharged. Second, where the contemnor is an officer of the court, disobedience is treated as an attack on the court's authority by someone in a privileged position, and his Honour expressly held that his age and experience counted heavily against him.

In Re Cool Breeze Clothing (No 1) [2026] VSC 530, Attiwill J refused leave to file an expert report tendered during the trial, applying Aon. The explanation for delay was found inadequate and s 65G of the Civil Procedure Act had not been complied with.

In Keycon Pty Ltd v Modi [2026] VSC 533, Gray J refused leave to appeal from VCAT, holding that a refused adjournment was no denial of procedural fairness where the applicant had not prepared for a hearing listed seven months in advance, and had not identified on appeal what it would have argued had the adjournment been granted.

Read together: an order ignored, a report served too late, and an adjournment sought the night before. Substantive merit rescued none of them.
