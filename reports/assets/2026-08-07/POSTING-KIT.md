## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; optionally attach slide 1)

The High Court has just expanded what "lawful interests" means, and the consequences reach well beyond criminal practice.

In Potter (a pseudonym) v The King [2026] HCA 25, delivered 5 August, a man convicted in South Australia of raping his sleeping wife appealed on the basis that a covert recording used against him was unlawfully obtained. A month before the second offence, his wife had recorded herself confronting him. He admitted the conduct.

Surveillance devices legislation generally prohibits recording a private conversation without the consent of the parties. The relevant exception permits recording where it is reasonably necessary for the protection of the lawful interests of the person making it. The appellant's argument was, in substance, that protecting a lawful interest means protecting a legal position, and that a recording made for personal resolve does not qualify.

The Court, unanimously, disagreed. The complainant gave evidence she made the recording to convince herself never to return to him. The Court held that replaying it served to remind her to avoid exposure to the real risk of being raped, and that the interest of a person in not being raped is an interest deserving of the very strongest protection. Appeal dismissed.

Three implications worth sitting with.

First, lawful interests are not confined to litigation or to preserving evidence. Personal safety and bodily autonomy are squarely within the concept.

Second, practitioners in family, criminal and personal safety matters now have appellate authority on a question clients ask constantly: can I record him? The answer remains fact-dependent, but the frame has shifted.

Third, the decision sits at an uncomfortable intersection with privacy. Surveillance devices laws exist for good reason, and this judgment does not open the gate to recording generally. It identifies a category of interest strong enough to justify it.

For anyone advising in this space, read the reasons in full before you advise. The margins matter.
