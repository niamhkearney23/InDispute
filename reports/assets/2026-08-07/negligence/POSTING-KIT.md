## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Modern negligence law rests on a snail nobody ever saw.

Paisley, August 1928. May Donoghue's friend buys her a ginger beer at the Wellmeadow Cafe. Dark opaque bottle. She drinks about half, the rest is poured out, and the decomposed remains of a snail come with it.

Here is the problem. Her friend bought it, so she had no contract with the cafe. And the rule at the time was that a manufacturer owed nothing to someone it had never dealt with. On the law as it stood, she had no claim against anybody.

The House of Lords, by a bare majority, said otherwise. Lord Atkin: you must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour. Your neighbour being anyone so closely and directly affected that you ought to have them in mind.

The twist they do not always tell you in first year: the snail was never proven. The Lords were deciding a preliminary question of law, whether there was a claim if the facts were true. The trial never happened. Stevenson died, his executors settled for a reported two hundred pounds, and the most important case in the common law of negligence has no findings of fact at all.

Then the question became how far it went. Food and drink only? Sealed containers only?

Adelaide, June 1931. Dr Richard Grant buys woollen underwear. The manufacturer left excess sulphite in the fabric and never washed it out. Itching by the evening, then dermatitis across his whole body, then seventeen weeks in bed.

The Privy Council applied Donoghue and settled the question. The principle covers goods that reach the consumer in the form they left the manufacturer, with no reasonable chance of inspection in between. Grant never proved how the sulphite got there. He did not have to. The defect itself was enough.

That is why every product you own comes with a duty attached, owed by someone you have never met.

*Donoghue v Stevenson* [1932] AC 562
*Grant v Australian Knitting Mills* [1936] AC 85

## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; attach slide 1)

The most important case in the common law of negligence has no findings of fact.

Most lawyers can recite Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562. Paisley, 1928, a ginger beer, a decomposed snail, an opaque bottle. Mrs Donoghue had no contract with the cafe because her friend bought the drink, and on the law as it stood a manufacturer owed nothing to a stranger.

The House of Lords, by a bare majority, held otherwise, and Lord Atkin gave us the neighbour principle: reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure those so closely and directly affected that you ought to have them in contemplation.

What gets left out of the lecture is that the Lords were answering a preliminary question of law. If the facts were true, was there a cause of action? The trial that would have established those facts never took place. Stevenson died, his executors settled for a reported two hundred pounds, and whether there was ever a snail in that bottle remains unknown.

Four years later the Privy Council answered the question Donoghue had left open. In Grant v Australian Knitting Mills [1936] AC 85, a doctor in Adelaide developed severe dermatitis from woollen underwear containing excess sulphite left in during manufacture. Seventeen weeks in bed.

The Privy Council held the principle was not confined to food, drink or sealed containers. It applies wherever goods reach the consumer in the form they left the manufacturer with no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination. The retailer was liable in contract, the manufacturer in tort, and critically Grant was not required to prove the mechanism of the negligence. The defect in a product made by a controlled process supported the inference.

Two observations for anyone who thinks doctrine is dry.

First, the reach of a principle is usually settled by an unglamorous case that follows it. Donoghue is famous. Grant did the structural work.

Second, both cases turned on a claimant who could not sue in contract. That is worth remembering every time someone treats privity as a minor technicality.

An enormous amount of modern consumer protection descends from a snail nobody saw and a rash nobody expected.
