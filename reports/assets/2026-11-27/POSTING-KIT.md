**LinkedIn post:**

A 1978 divorce settlement is still being litigated in 2026, and the legal question is a good one.

Cher sued Mary Bono, Sonny Bono&rsquo;s widow, in October 2021, claiming a continuing right to 50% of the heirs&rsquo; income from songs Sonny wrote, including <i>I Got You Babe</i>, <i>The Beat Goes On</i> and <i>Baby Don&rsquo;t Go</i>. The heirs had served a notice of termination, the US mechanism that allows songwriters and their successors to reclaim American publishing rights, and the argument was that reclaiming those rights swept away Cher&rsquo;s share.

Judge John A. Kronstadt of the Central District of California entered final judgment on 26 November 2025, ruling largely in Cher&rsquo;s favour and awarding her more than US$187,000 plus interest for withheld publishing royalties, on the basis that she remains entitled to half the income from songs written before 1 February 1974. His earlier reasoning was that a notice of termination cannot affect a contractual right to receive financial compensation given in exchange for releasing claims arising out of the marriage.

In July 2026 he denied Cher&rsquo;s request for attorneys&rsquo; fees. She appealed. On 24 November 2026 Mary Bono&rsquo;s lawyers filed her opening brief in the Ninth Circuit, arguing that federal copyright law gives the heirs the reclaimed publishing interests free of Cher&rsquo;s claim, and contending that the decision below would let a publisher evade the 1976 Act&rsquo;s termination provisions by drafting a separate royalty clause. Her brief also accepts that Cher &ldquo;played an integral role in Sonny&rsquo;s success&rdquo;. The two appeals are now consolidated and undecided.

For anyone learning commercial litigation, the discipline on display is characterisation. Before you apply a statutory override, define the right in front of you. A promise to pay a share of income is not the same thing as a transfer of the asset that generates it, and the drafting that creates each one looks very different on the page.

If the Ninth Circuit accepts the appellant&rsquo;s framing, every royalty clause written alongside a copyright transfer becomes a drafting question rather than a property question. Which approach do you think gives commercial parties more certainty?
