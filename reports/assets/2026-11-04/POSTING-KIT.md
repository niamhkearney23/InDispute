A class action against RAMS Financial Group settled after the evidence had already been heard at a concurrent trial. The parties were content. That was not the end of it.

Under s 33V of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth), a representative proceeding cannot be settled without the Court&rsquo;s approval. So Lee J asked the questions the parties had already answered for themselves: is this fair and reasonable for group members, and as between group members, and are the deductions from the fund just?

The settlement was approved. The funding commission and the reimbursement of legal costs were approved as deductions. The reasoning is the part worth keeping: the funding return was assessed by reference to the risks assumed and the capital exposed, with hindsight avoided and comparisons to headline commission percentages treated as limited. The Court also addressed the limits of a representative applicant&rsquo;s authority to impose contractual obligations upon absent group members, and preserved group members&rsquo; ability to seek a costs assessment.

The group here was small, identifiable, and silent. The absence of objections mattered, but it did not do the Court&rsquo;s work for it.

**LinkedIn post:**

Settlement is usually where a case goes quiet. In a class action, it is where the Court starts paying attention.

On 30 October 2026 the Federal Court approved the settlement of a representative proceeding brought against RAMS Financial Group: Top Ryde Financial Services Pty Ltd v RAMS Financial Group Pty Limited (Settlement Approval) [2026] FCA 1652 (Lee J).

The procedural posture is unusual and worth noting. The settlement was reached after evidence had already been heard at a concurrent trial, with a related proceeding continuing to judgment. That meant the assessment of litigation risk was informed by evidence and findings in the related proceeding rather than by forecast alone.

Three things stand out for anyone working in or near class actions:

1. Approval is substantive, not administrative. Section 33V required the Court to be satisfied the settlement was fair and reasonable in the interests of group members, and as between themselves. The class was small and identifiable, and the absence of objections was weighed rather than assumed to be decisive.

2. Funding returns are judged by risk, not by outcome. The funding commission and reimbursement of legal costs were assessed as just under s 33V(2) by reference to the risks assumed and the capital exposed, with hindsight avoided and comparisons with headline commission percentages recognised as having limits. Funding equalisation was addressed, and group members&rsquo; ability to seek a costs assessment was preserved.

3. Absent group members are not parties to a private bargain. The Court addressed the limits of a representative applicant&rsquo;s authority to impose contractual obligations upon absent group members, and gave an affected group member the opportunity to dispute a revised proportional allocation before distribution.

For students: this is the case to read if you have only ever met Part IVA as a topic heading. It shows the supervisory function doing real work.
