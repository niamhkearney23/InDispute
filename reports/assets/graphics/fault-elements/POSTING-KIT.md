## Why this one

A single graphic has to earn a save on its own, with no swipe to explain it. So it has to be a thing people get wrong and want to keep.

This is that thing. Students learn "the fault element" as though an offence has one. It does not. Every physical element has its own, and here they run all the way down: intention, then recklessness, then nothing at all. Ko turns on exactly this, because the whole appeal was about a jury being nudged from the second one into the first.

It is also completely safe. No parties, no outcome, no allegation about anybody. It states a provision and the fault elements attaching to it. There is nothing here that can be wrong about a real person.

**Design note:** the weight of each chip carries the meaning. Solid navy for intention, outlined for recklessness, dashed and grey for absolute liability. The visual descends as the required state of mind does, so the graphic reads before it is read.

## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Most people learn this wrong, and it is the kind of wrong that costs marks.

An offence does not have "a" fault element. Every physical element has its own, and they are frequently different.

Take importing a commercial quantity of a border controlled drug, section 307.1(1) of the Criminal Code.

**That the person imports a substance.** Fault element: intention. They meant to import it. Not that they should have known, not that they took a chance. They meant to.

**That the substance is a border controlled drug.** Fault element: recklessness. They saw a substantial risk it was and went ahead anyway. They do not need to have known what it was.

**That the quantity is a commercial quantity.** Absolute liability. No state of mind required at all. The quantity is the quantity.

So a single count can require a different mental state for each thing the prosecution has to prove, and the accused's state of mind can be different for each one at the same moment.

That is not a technicality. It is the whole architecture of Chapter 2 of the Code, and it is why an appeal can succeed on nothing more than a judge blurring two of them in front of a jury. Which is precisely what happened in *The King v Ko* last month.

If you are studying criminal law, the habit worth building is this: never ask "what is the fault element". Break the offence into its physical elements first, then ask what attaches to each one. Do it in that order every time and you will stop losing marks to a question that looked simple.

Save it for exam block.
