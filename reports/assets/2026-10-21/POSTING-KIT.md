**Instagram caption (copy-paste):**

Working from home is usually framed as a culture debate. In the Federal Court it is a legal question, and this week it got one step closer to trial.

A woman employed as a casual by a labour hire business was placed with a customer in an accounts receivable role. Before surgery, and while recovering from it, she asked for a temporary work from home arrangement as a reasonable adjustment. The customer maintained the role had to be performed on site. Her assignment was terminated.

She says that was disability discrimination, by the customer and by the labour hire business that managed her request. The labour hire business says it had no control over her role on assignment. The Australian Human Rights Commission terminated her complaint, which meant she needed the Court&rsquo;s leave to commence proceedings under s 46PO(3A)(a) of the Australian Human Rights Commission Act 1986 (Cth).

On 21 October 2026, Banks-Smith J granted that leave. The Court recorded a dispute as to whether the on-site requirement was an inherent requirement or the customer&rsquo;s preference, found factual issues that should be resolved at trial, and noted a reasonable argument within the principles for leave. A proceeding against the customer was already on foot, and the claims overlap.

Learn this: a discrimination complaint terminated by the Commission may still proceed if the applicant shows a reasonable argument fit for trial.

The case to watch is the one behind it. Inherent requirements have always been the pressure point in disability discrimination. Remote work turns that pressure up, because the answer is no longer obvious from the job title.

**LinkedIn post:**

A quiet decision from the Federal Court on 21 October 2026 is worth five minutes of any employment lawyer&rsquo;s attention.

The facts are modern and very ordinary. A woman was employed as a casual by a labour hire business and placed with a customer on assignment, doing accounts receivable. Before surgery, and while recovering from it, she asked for a temporary work from home arrangement as a reasonable adjustment. The customer maintained that the role had to be performed on site. Her assignment was terminated.

She alleges disability discrimination by the customer, and by the labour hire business, both in how it managed her request to work from home and as a party she says was directly involved in the customer&rsquo;s conduct. The labour hire business contends it had no control over her role while she was on assignment.

Because the Australian Human Rights Commission had terminated her complaint, she needed leave to commence proceedings under s 46PO(3A)(a) of the Australian Human Rights Commission Act 1986 (Cth).

In Darlington-Thomas v Elgas Limited [2026] FCA 1566, Banks-Smith J granted leave. The Court&rsquo;s catchwords identify the live dispute: whether the on-site requirement was an inherent requirement or the customer&rsquo;s preference. Those are factual issues that should be resolved at trial. The applicant had a reasonable argument within the principles for leave, a proceeding against the customer was already on foot, and there is factual and legal overlap between all the claims.

Three things I would flag for practitioners and for students:

1. Leave is a gateway, not a verdict. The threshold is a reasonable argument, not a concluded view on contested facts.

2. Inherent requirements are becoming harder to assert by assertion. If on-site attendance is genuinely essential, the evidence needs to show it rather than the job description asserting it.

3. Triangular employment keeps producing hard liability questions. One entity holds the contract. Another shapes the day to day work. Discrimination law has to deal with both, and &ldquo;we had no control&rdquo; is a position that will be tested.
