## 📸 THE IMAGE WELL, read this first

Every story slide (2 to 5) has a bordered block sitting exactly where the reference account puts its photo.

- **Size: 800 × 420 pixels**
- **Position: horizontally centred, and the block's own border shows you the edges**
- It is **postable exactly as it is**, because the number and the verdict sit inside it
- Drop an 800 × 420 picture over that block and **nothing else moves**

So you can run this today with no images at all, then post it again next month with images, and it is the same deck.

**Slide 4 is the one. A big industrial dough mixer under that headline is the funniest image on the account.**

### ⚠️ What NOT to put in there

**No photographs of the people in these cases.** Not Mr Guss, not Mr Ko, not Mr Lewis, not anyone from Harvey Norman.

This is the one place where copying the reference account would actually hurt you. They put Zuckerberg and Altman on slides, and that is a different thing: those are public figures being discussed as executives. Ours are litigants. One of them is awaiting a retrial and is presumed innocent. Putting his face next to "101 packages of drugs" is not a look you want, and it is not a risk worth taking for a picture.

**Objects, places and screens only.** That is also why it is funnier.

**On sourcing:** your own photos are always safest. If you use a stock library, check the licence actually covers commercial social use, and do not lift press photography from news sites, which is licensed to them and not to you.

## The tone dial

I wrote "Court had a month." Dry rather than chatty, because it still has to sound like Lawgistics and "Girl, did you miss the tea" is a different brand.

If you want it warmer, that one line is the only thing that changes:

- **"Court had a month."** ← built, driest, most premium
- "Four things that actually happened in court."
- "Court was busy. Here is what you missed."
- "Did you miss the court tea?" ← chattiest

Swap the cover line, everything else carries either voice.

## Instagram caption (copy-paste)

Court had a month. Four decisions, and one of them is genuinely funny.

**That ad just cost Harvey Norman $55 million.** Sixty months interest free, no deposit, the one you have seen a thousand times. All of it true. What it did not mention is that you had to take out a credit card, with an establishment fee and monthly account service fees. At least $537 in fees, on the interest free deal. $35 million against Harvey Norman, $20 million against Latitude, and ninety days of corrective advertising on their own home pages.

**A password cost a man seven days in jail.** A former solicitor was ordered to hand the regulator's appointed manager the password to the email account he ran his law practice from. He refused. Through two judges. Through a failed appeal. At the penalty hearing his own IT consultant gave evidence that complying would take about ten minutes. He handed it over that afternoon. Conviction recorded, 45 days imprisonment with 38 suspended, seven to be served. He is 88.

An appeal does not stay an order. Until it is set aside, you obey it. That principle is old, and it is unforgiving, and this is what it looks like applied to someone who spent his career knowing it.

**101 packages of drugs, inside a commercial dough mixer.** Shipped from Toronto to Sydney. Canadian authorities found them before it left, took them out, put an inert substance in and let the mixer sail anyway. The conviction that followed has now been quashed over one sentence a judge did not say to the jury: that even if it found the accused saw a real chance of something in there, it still had to decide whether he actually meant to import it. New trial ordered. Nothing decided against him.

**And a partner sued his own law firm.** He was 66, told he would be excluded from the firm's proposed float, and months later the partners voted to expel him. He sued for age discrimination. The firm tried to have the whole thing thrown out before trial and failed. To be clear, nothing has been decided about whether any discrimination happened. The ruling decided one thing: the case can be heard.
