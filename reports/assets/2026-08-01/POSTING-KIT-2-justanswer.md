## LinkedIn post (copy-paste; optionally attach slide 1)

Two dollars. That was the promise. Ten million dollars was the price.

In ACCC v JustAnswer LLC [2026] FCA 871, Justice Snaden ordered JustAnswer to pay $10 million in penalties for misleading Australian consumers. Between November 2022 and August 2025, the company's website chat widget told users it was sending them to a page to join "for only AU$2 (fully refundable)". Consumers who accepted were in fact enrolled in an ongoing subscription costing between $45 and $75 per month. Separately, from June 2022 to February 2024, the company falsely represented that it was approved by or affiliated with the Fair Work Ombudsman.

JustAnswer admitted the contraventions. Beyond the penalty, the Court ordered consumer redress, corrective notices, a compliance program and injunctions restraining similar conduct.

Two things make this more than another penalty judgment.

First, the redress. Refunds for affected consumers turn a regulatory outcome into a remediation exercise, and the operational burden of that often exceeds the headline number.

Second, the timing. On 2 July 2026 Parliament passed the Competition and Consumer Amendment (Unfair Trading Practices) Act. From 1 July 2027, subscription traps, drip pricing and dark patterns become separately prohibited under the Australian Consumer Law, alongside a new general prohibition on unfair trading practices. Conduct that today requires the ACCC to prove misleading representations will soon be captured directly.

For anyone advising businesses with an online funnel, the next twelve months are the window. Audit the signup flow, the cancellation flow, and every price that appears at checkout but not before. If a reasonable consumer could finish your funnel without understanding what they will be charged and when, you have work to do.
