# FEATURED: Potter (a pseudonym) v The King [2026] HCA 25

## 1. Quick Summary

- **Court:** High Court of Australia
- **Bench:** Gageler CJ, Gordon, Steward, Jagot and Beech-Jones JJ (unanimous)
- **Practice Area:** Evidence / Criminal law / Surveillance devices / Family violence
- **Decision Date:** 5 August 2026
- **Citation:** *Potter (a pseudonym) v The King* [2026] HCA 25

**30-word summary:** A woman's covert recording of her husband admitting to raping her while she slept was lawful, being reasonably necessary to protect her lawful interests. Appeal dismissed, convictions stand.

## 2. The Story

A man in South Australia was convicted of raping his wife on two occasions while she was asleep. The jury could not reach a verdict on two further counts. He is serving nine and a half years.

A month before the last of those offences, his wife recorded a conversation on her phone in which she confronted him about the assaults. He admitted them. That recording was used at his trial.

He appealed, arguing the recording was unlawfully made. Surveillance devices legislation generally prohibits recording a private conversation without the consent of the parties to it. There is an exception where the recording is reasonably necessary for the protection of the lawful interests of the person making it.

His argument, in substance: a "lawful interest" means a legal position, and a recording made for personal resolve is not that.

The complainant's evidence was that she made the recording to convince herself never to go back to him, no matter what happened.

The High Court, unanimously, dismissed the appeal.

## 3. Why This Matters

"Can I record him?" is one of the most commonly asked questions in family law, criminal practice and personal safety matters, and until now the answer leaned heavily on caution. This decision does not make covert recording generally lawful, but it identifies personal safety and bodily autonomy as interests capable of engaging the exception.

It also matters for evidence strategy. Recordings of this kind are often the only corroboration available in offending that happens in private. A ruling that they can be lawfully made, and therefore admitted, changes what is realistically provable.

## 4. Key Legal Principles

- Surveillance devices legislation prohibits recording private conversations without consent, subject to a "lawful interests" exception.
- A lawful interest is not confined to preserving evidence for litigation or protecting a legal position.
- Protection of bodily autonomy and integrity falls within the concept.
- The Court reasoned that replaying the recording served to remind the complainant to avoid exposure to the real risk of being raped.
- In the Court's words: the interest of a person in not being raped is an interest deserving of the very strongest protection.
- The assessment is fact-specific; the purpose for which the recording was made matters.

## 5. Practical Takeaway

If you're acting for a client tomorrow: the answer to "can I record him" is still "it depends", but the ground has shifted. Where a client is recording to protect their own safety, and can articulate that purpose, the exception is live. Advise clients to be able to explain, contemporaneously if possible, why they recorded. Do not generalise this to commercial or workplace recording, where the lawful interest analysis is very different. And read the full reasons before advising: this decision turns on its facts and its statutory context.

# Potter v The King: High Court Rules Covert Recording by a Victim Was Lawful

## The decision

On 5 August 2026, the High Court of Australia unanimously dismissed an appeal in *Potter (a pseudonym) v The King* [2026] HCA 25, holding that a covert recording made by a woman of her husband admitting to sexual offences against her was lawfully made and properly admitted at his trial.

## Background

The appellant was convicted in South Australia of raping his wife on two occasions while she slept. A month before the last of those offences, the complainant recorded a conversation in which she confronted him and he admitted the conduct. She gave evidence that she made the recording to convince herself never to return to him.

## The legal question

Surveillance devices legislation generally makes it an offence to record a private conversation without the consent of the parties. The relevant exception applies where the recording is reasonably necessary for the protection of the lawful interests of the person making it. The appeal turned on whether a recording made for the complainant's own resolve and safety fell within that exception.

## What the Court held

The Court held that it did. The reasoning was expressed in terms of protecting bodily autonomy and integrity: replaying the recording reminded the complainant to avoid exposure to the real risk of being raped. The Court described the interest of a person in not being raped as an interest deserving of the very strongest protection. The appeal was dismissed and the convictions stand.

## What it means

**For criminal and family practitioners.** There is now High Court authority that lawful interests extend beyond legal positions to personal safety. Advice on covert recording should be updated, while remaining fact-specific.

**For victims and support services.** Recordings made for safety reasons are not automatically unlawful, and may be admissible.

**For privacy generally.** This is not a general licence to record. Surveillance devices laws continue to apply; the decision identifies a category of interest strong enough to engage an existing exception.
