# The People in the Cars

*Draft for the Lawgistics blog. Follow-up to "The Law and the People It Leaves Behind" (6 Mar). Written to run under Niamh Kearney's byline.*

---

A few months ago I wrote about walking to work in Kuala Lumpur. Past Ferraris, luxury condos and five star hotels, and then, a few streets away, past people sleeping under bridges. I wrote that the cars had started to feel less like symbols of achievement and more like a lack of empathy.

I have been thinking about that line ever since, and I do not think it is right.

The problem is not that it was harsh. It is that it was easy.

I did not know one thing about the people in those cars. Not how they earned it, not what they do with it, not who depends on them, not where they started. I watched a car go past and I wrote a whole character. That is precisely the reasoning a law degree spends four years training us out of. We are taught to ask what the evidence establishes. I had no evidence. I had a car.

Most of those people were doing something completely ordinary. Driving to work. Going home. Thinking about a meeting, or a parent, or nothing at all. Someone in one of those cars almost certainly grew up with far less than I did. Someone in one of those cars probably gives away more in a year than I will earn in five. I could not tell which, because none of that is visible from a footpath.

There is a second problem with what I wrote, and it took me longer to see.

If the reason people cannot access the legal system is that wealthy individuals lack empathy, then the solution is for wealthy individuals to feel worse about themselves. That is an extremely comfortable conclusion. It asks nothing of me, nothing of the profession and nothing of the system. It turns a structural problem into a morality play, and morality plays are satisfying precisely because they finish.

The reason a person cannot enforce their rights is not that someone else owns a nice car.

It is that legal services are expensive, and priced in a way almost nobody can plan for. It is that legal aid is funded well below what the demand requires. It is that court processes are written in language which assumes a lawyer is the one reading them. It is that the parties who use the system most often are also the ones best placed to shape it. None of that is caused by an individual driver, and none of it is repaired by resenting one.

The discomfort was right. I aimed it at the wrong thing.

What actually moves access to justice is unglamorous. Duty schemes at the door of the court. Plain language forms. Fee structures a person can understand before they are committed. Pro bono hours that are counted and protected rather than mentioned in a brochure. Funding decisions made years in advance by people whose names nobody will ever know. It is slow, administrative work, and it is almost completely invisible from the street.

There is a lesson in this for law students specifically, and I say that as one.

Moral clarity feels like insight. It arrives quickly, it reads well, and it makes you sound as though you have understood something. Real analysis is slower, less flattering, and usually ends somewhere less quotable. The first version of my argument was the better sentence. The second one is the more useful one.

I am not walking back what prompted it. The contrast between those streets is real, it should be uncomfortable, and I do not think anyone should get used to it. Being unsettled by it is a reasonable response and probably a necessary one for anyone planning to practise.

I just no longer think the person in the car put the person under the bridge there.

They were going about their day. So was I.

**Niamh Kearney**

## Notes for you

**Word count:** about 660, so it sits close to the original.

**Title alternates**, if you want a different one:
- Second Thoughts About the Ferraris
- What I Got Wrong About the Cars
- The Easy Conclusion

**The CEO passage is not referenced.** The original post describes an identifiable person, a female CEO of a named industry and firm size, and attributes to her that she ignores her guilt, is motivated by money, sees her children once a year, and chose a jurisdiction for weaker regulation. Repeating any of that in a second post republishes it, and republication is its own act. Your blog, your call, but I would not build on it, and I would not put it on a carousel.

**Why this one works.** It does the thing almost no one does online, which is revise a published view without deleting the original. It reads as confident rather than defensive, because it concedes the reasoning and keeps the underlying concern. For an audience of law students it also models the difference between a good sentence and a good argument, which is a genuinely useful thing to show them.

**It would make a strong carousel.** Six slides: the original line, "it was easy, not harsh", "I had no evidence, I had a car", the morality play problem, what actually changes access, "they were going about their day, so was I". Say the word.
