# White Oak v Insurance Australia: The "Without Prejudice" Label That Didn't Stick

## 1. Quick Summary

- **Court:** Federal Court of Australia
- **Judge:** Justice Thawley
- **Practice Area:** Insurance / Commercial Litigation / Evidence & Privilege
- **Decision Date:** Published in the Federal Court's 29 July 2026 release window (delivered earlier in proceeding NSD 1039 of 2021) — verify against the sealed judgment before republication
- **Citation:** *White Oak Commercial Finance Europe (Non-Levered) Ltd v Insurance Australia Ltd (Without Prejudice Privilege)* [2026] FCA 769

**30-word summary:** In the Greensill insurance mega-litigation, Thawley J refused to cloak informal settlement-adjacent messages in without prejudice privilege: no genuine negotiation, no compromise, no privilege — and irrelevant documents don't get produced anyway.

## 2. The Story

Remember Greensill? The supply-chain finance giant that collapsed in 2021 and took half the trade credit insurance market to court with it. White Oak, a lender left holding Greensill-linked debts, is suing Insurance Australia Ltd (IAL), saying a policy issued through IAL's agent, BCC Trade Credit, should cover its losses.

This round wasn't about the money. It was about documents. IAL asked the Court to make White Oak hand over a bundle of material — including around twelve messages and a draft term sheet created while the parties were circling a possible settlement.

White Oak said: those are settlement communications, they're protected.

Justice Thawley looked at the actual messages. Some were just general chat about how the parties might approach the dispute — no offers, no admissions, no give-and-take. Others simply weren't relevant to any live issue. Result: no protection needed, and no production either.

## 3. Why This Matters

"Without prejudice" is the most over-used phrase in commercial disputes — and this judgment is a reminder that courts look at substance, not labels. Privilege only attaches to communications that are genuinely part of an attempt to compromise a dispute. Vibes-level discussion about the dispute doesn't qualify.

For insurers, lenders and anyone in the Greensill fallout, it's also a signal: the Federal Court is actively policing what gets shielded in these enormous document fights. And for every litigator, it's a two-step framework worth memorising — relevance first, privilege second. If a document isn't directly relevant, the privilege argument never even starts.

## 4. Key Legal Principles

- Without prejudice privilege protects communications made in a genuine attempt to negotiate the resolution of an existing or contemplated dispute — not every dispute-adjacent communication.
- Indicators the Court looked for: actual negotiation, discussion of resolution, reference to possible litigation, admissions, and an observable element of compromise or intention to offer one.
- Messages canvassing only a "general approach" the parties might take to their dispute do not attract the privilege.
- Relevance is the gateway: documents not directly relevant to an issue in the proceeding need not be produced, making privilege questions unnecessary.
- The party asserting privilege carries the evidentiary burden of substantiating it, document by document.

## 5. Practical Takeaway

If you're acting for a client tomorrow: stop assuming a "without prejudice" header does anything. Before asserting the privilege, map each document against the actual negotiation — when the dispute crystallised, what was being offered, where the element of compromise is. Prepare affidavit evidence that substantiates the claim per document, not in bulk. And when resisting production, argue relevance first; it's often the cleaner kill. When drafting settlement communications, make the negotiation explicit: reference the dispute, the proposed compromise, and the litigation risk being addressed.

# Without Prejudice Privilege: Federal Court Confirms Labels Aren't Enough in White Oak v Insurance Australia [2026] FCA 769

## The Greensill fallout produces another key evidence ruling

The collapse of Greensill in 2021 spawned some of the largest insurance litigation Australia has seen. In the latest instalment, *White Oak Commercial Finance Europe (Non-Levered) Ltd v Insurance Australia Ltd (Without Prejudice Privilege)* [2026] FCA 769, Justice Thawley of the Federal Court delivered a sharp reminder about one of litigation's most misunderstood doctrines: without prejudice privilege.

## Background: lender v insurer

White Oak, a commercial finance house, claims against Insurance Australia Ltd (IAL) for losses connected to Greensill-linked receivables, under a trade credit insurance policy said to have been issued by BCC Trade Credit Pty Ltd as IAL's authorised representative. The proceeding (NSD 1039 of 2021) is one of several coverage battles working through the Federal Court.

By an amended interlocutory application, IAL sought production of documents held by White Oak — including a set of roughly twelve messages and a draft term sheet created while the parties explored resolving the dispute. White Oak resisted, asserting without prejudice privilege.

## What is without prejudice privilege?

Without prejudice privilege prevents evidence of settlement negotiations, made in relation to existing or contemplated proceedings, from being admitted into evidence. The policy is simple: parties should be able to speak freely when trying to settle, without their concessions being weaponised later.

But the protection has boundaries — and this judgment maps them.

## What the Court decided

Justice Thawley examined the disputed communications and found key groups fell outside the privilege because they:

- were "broadly directed towards a general approach the parties might take to their dispute";
- did not constitute negotiations or directly discuss any resolution;
- contained no reference to the possibility of litigation and no admissions; and
- displayed no observable element of compromise, nor any intention to make an offer of compromise.

In short: they were dispute-adjacent, not settlement-directed. Other documents failed at an earlier hurdle — they were not directly relevant to any issue in the proceeding, so production was refused without the privilege question ever arising.

## Why this decision matters

**For litigators.** The case confirms courts will scrutinise the substance of each communication. The party claiming privilege must prove, with evidence, that each document formed part of a genuine attempt to compromise a dispute. Blanket claims and reflexive "without prejudice" headers will not survive contact with a judge who reads the documents.

**For insurers and insureds.** Coverage disputes generate enormous document sets and hard-fought interlocutory skirmishes. Knowing what is — and is not — protected shapes how you communicate from the first reservation-of-rights letter onward.

**For businesses.** If you want your settlement discussions protected, make them look like settlement discussions: identify the dispute, engage with resolution, and put an actual compromise on the table.

## Practical checklist

1. Argue relevance first; privilege second.
2. Substantiate privilege claims document by document, with affidavit evidence.
3. In negotiations, make the elements explicit: the dispute, the litigation risk, the proposed compromise.
4. Audit "without prejudice" habits — the label alone achieves nothing.

# Elvin v Fair Work Ombudsman: The Full Court's Playbook on Underpayments, Self-Represented Litigants and DOCAs

## 1. Quick Summary

- **Court:** Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia
- **Judges:** Collier, McDonald and Vandongen JJ
- **Practice Area:** Employment / Industrial Law, with an insolvency crossover
- **Decision Date:** 22 July 2026 (published in the Federal Court's late-July window; some digests record 21 July — verify against the judgment)
- **Citation:** *Elvin v Fair Work Ombudsman* [2026] FCAFC 92

**30-word summary:** Full Court partly allows appeal in FWO underpayment case involving massage therapists: award classification, accessorial liability, fairness to self-represented litigants, and whether the FWO counts as a DOCA creditor.

## 2. The Story

The Fair Work Ombudsman went after a massage business over underpaid massage therapists, and won at trial — including findings against an individual said to be personally involved in the contraventions.

The appeal threw almost everything at the wall. Were massage therapists really covered as "Health Professional Level 1" under the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2010? Was the trial unfair because the appellant ran the case without a lawyer, and the judge applied technical evidence rules — the ones about failing to call witnesses or put your case to the other side — against a self-represented person? Was the judge biased in how the hearing was managed?

And then a curveball from insolvency land: when the employing company did a deal with its creditors (a deed of company arrangement), did the FWO count as a "creditor" bound by that deal?

The Full Court allowed the appeal in part, and dismissed the FWO's cross-appeal.

## 3. Why This Matters

Underpayment enforcement is the FWO's flagship activity, and this is a Full Court decision touching the pressure points that decide these cases: how you classify workers under an award, when individuals wear personal liability as accessories, and how far courts must accommodate self-represented parties before technical evidence rules bite.

The DOCA question is the sleeper issue. Whether a regulator pursuing penalties and underpayments is a "creditor" under a deed of company arrangement sits at the junction of employment enforcement and insolvency — and matters enormously to directors hoping a DOCA draws a line under workplace liabilities. Spoiler: don't bet the house on it.

## 4. Key Legal Principles

- Award classification is decided by matching the substance of duties against the classification definitions — here, whether massage therapists fell within Health Professional Level 1 under the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2010.
- Accessorial liability under the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) exposes individuals "involved in" contraventions to personal findings and penalties.
- The rules in *Browne v Dunn* and *Jones v Dunkel* must be applied with care where a party is self-represented; their application can ground appeal points about procedural fairness.
- Bias challenges based on case management and evidentiary rulings face a high bar.
- A company's deed of company arrangement does not straightforwardly capture the FWO; whether the regulator is a "creditor" for DOCA purposes was squarely in issue.

## 5. Practical Takeaway

If you're acting for a client tomorrow: in underpayment matters, audit the award classification first — it drives quantum, and it's contestable. Advising directors of struggling employers? Do not assume a DOCA extinguishes FWO exposure; personal accessorial liability survives corporate rescue mechanics, so deal with the regulator directly. If your opponent is self-represented, protect your win: put your case squarely in cross-examination and keep the trial scrupulously fair, because fairness points are exactly what appeal courts will entertain. Document classification reasoning contemporaneously.

# Elvin v Fair Work Ombudsman [2026] FCAFC 92: Underpayments, Award Classification and the DOCA Question

## A Full Court decision every employer should know about

The Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia — Justices Collier, McDonald and Vandongen — has handed down its decision in *Elvin v Fair Work Ombudsman* [2026] FCAFC 92, partly allowing an appeal in a Fair Work Ombudsman underpayment prosecution and dismissing the Ombudsman's cross-appeal. The case is a compact tour of the issues that decide modern wage-compliance litigation.

## The background

The Fair Work Ombudsman alleged that employees engaged as massage therapists were underpaid, in contravention of the Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth). A central battleground was classification: were the therapists properly treated as Health Professional Level 1 under the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2010? Classification determines minimum rates — get it wrong and every pay cycle compounds the error.

The trial also produced accessorial liability findings, exposing an individual involved in the contraventions to personal consequences, and the employing company's deed of company arrangement (DOCA) raised a rare crossover question: is the FWO a "creditor" bound by a DOCA?

## The appeal

The appellant, who had been self-represented at trial, challenged the outcome on several fronts:

- **Award classification** — whether the massage therapists fell within the Health Professional Level 1 definition;
- **Procedural fairness** — whether evidentiary rules such as *Browne v Dunn* (put your case to opposing witnesses) and *Jones v Dunkel* (inferences from unexplained failures to call evidence) were properly applied against a litigant without legal representation;
- **Apprehended bias** — directed at case management decisions, evidentiary rulings and the conduct of the hearing; and
- **The DOCA point** — the status of the FWO as a creditor.

The Full Court allowed the appeal in part. The FWO's cross-appeal was dismissed.

## Why the case matters

**Classification is the quantum engine.** Most underpayment disputes are really classification disputes. This decision shows appellate courts will engage closely with how duties map onto award definitions — and that employers need contemporaneous, defensible classification reasoning.

**Self-represented litigants change the fairness calculus.** Courts must apply technical evidence rules carefully when one side has no lawyer. For represented parties, the lesson is defensive: run a scrupulously fair trial, put your case squarely, and protect the judgment from fairness-based appeal points.

**DOCAs are not a force field.** Directors sometimes assume a deed of company arrangement resolves everything, including regulatory claims. The Full Court's engagement with whether the FWO is a DOCA creditor shows the question is live and technical — and accessorial liability attaches to individuals personally, entirely outside the company's insolvency arrangements.

## What employers and advisers should do now

1. Audit award classifications for every role, and record the reasoning.
2. Treat FWO engagement as a personal-risk issue for managers and directors, not just a corporate one.
3. In insolvency scenarios, get specific advice on how regulatory claims interact with the DOCA — before relying on it.
4. Against self-represented opponents, over-invest in procedural fairness.
